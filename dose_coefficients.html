    Dose coefficients for members of the public

    A dose coefficient is the committed dose per becquerel taken into the body: the equivalent dose to an organ or tissue, or the effective dose, received over the 50 years after an adult’s intake, or up to age 70 after a child’s. This page calculates them the way the ICRP does, from the ICRP’s biokinetic models (where the radionuclide goes in the body and how long it stays) and dosimetric models (how much of the energy emitted in one place is absorbed in another), for intakes by ingestion and inhalation (and, in the ICRP 103 system, direct uptake to blood) at the six reference ages of the ICRP’s publications for members of the public: 3 months, 1, 5, 10 and 15 years, and adults. Choose the system, the radionuclide, the route and the chemical form on the left. The route External gives instead the dose rate of a person in contaminated air, water or soil, as the US Federal Guidance Reports 12 and 15 calculate it (see External exposure below). The button at the right end of the tabs gives the page the whole window, without the site’s header and footer; the kvot mark at the left of the tabs then goes to the home page, and the switch beside the button changes between light and dark. The page remembers the choice.

    The two systems

    The ICRP 60 system is the one of the ICRP’s 1990 Recommendations: the age-dependent models of Publications 56, 67, 69 and 71, whose dose coefficients Publication 72 tabulated and Publication 119 compiled. It is still the basis of many national regulations and of the IAEA Basic Safety Standards’ tables. The ICRP 103 system is the one of the 2007 Recommendations: new tissue weighting factors, voxel phantoms of reference persons of both sexes, the revised respiratory tract model of Publication 130, the alimentary tract model of Publication 100 and revised systemic models. For members of the public its coefficients are being published element by element: Part 1 (Publication 158, 2024) covers hydrogen to radium, Part 2 (the lanthanides and actinides) and Part 3 (35 more elements, beryllium to francium) were out for consultation as drafts when this page was made. The two systems give different numbers for the same intake, sometimes by a factor of several; the page tells you which one each result comes from.

    The ICRP 60 system as calculated here

    The ICRP calculated the Publication 72 coefficients with the software DCAL (Eckerman and Leggett, ORNL/TM-2001/190), which ORNL distributes with its data. This page reads DCAL’s own model and data files and reproduces its method:

    • Respiratory tract: the ICRP 66 model, Types F, M and S and the gas and vapour classes, with DCAL’s age-dependent deposition fractions for members of the public (1 µm AMAD by default).
    • Gastrointestinal tract: the ICRP 30 model (stomach, small intestine, upper and lower large intestine), absorption f1 from the small intestine with its age-dependent values.
    • Urinary bladder: emptied at 40, 32 and 12 d−1 at 3 months, 1 year and from 5 years (Publication 67).
    • Systemic models: the ones of Publications 56–71 as DCAL’s FGR-13 library holds them, with the transfer rates interpolated linearly in age, and the adult at 25 years for bone-seeking elements. Progeny follow the kinetics DCAL’s batch files give them: shared with the parent, or independent with the element-specific models for lead, radium, thorium and uranium chains, iodine from tellurium and noble gases.
    • Dosimetry: specific effective energies from the Cristy–Eckerman phantoms (newborn, 1, 5, 10 and 15 years, adult), the ICRP 30 bone and walled-organ absorbed fractions, and the ICRP 66 absorbed fractions of the airways, interpolated in age between the phantoms with the weights DCAL gives them.
    • Effective dose: the tissue weighting factors of Publication 60 with the remainder of Publication 72 (mass-weighted, with the splitting rule).

    The ICRP 103 system as calculated here

    • Respiratory tract: the revised Human Respiratory Tract Model of Publication 130 (particle transport of its Fig. 2.4, dissolution and the bound state), with the deposition of Publication 158 Table A.1 for environmental exposure (1 µm AMAD by default), the absorption types and material-specific parameters of each element, and the parent’s absorption parameters for progeny formed in the lungs.
    • Alimentary tract: the Human Alimentary Tract Model of Publication 100 with the age-specific transit rates of Publication 158 Table 2.10, absorption fA from the small intestine; food and drink pass the oesophagus 90 % by its fast transit and 10 % by its slow one, saliva and material cleared from the lungs by the slow one alone (section 2.3.2.3); material cleared from the lungs is absorbed with fr·fA, and activity secreted from tissues with the highest reference fA. An injection puts the activity into the systemic model’s blood at once.
    • Systemic models: transcribed from each element’s section of Publication 158 and of the Part 2 and Part 3 drafts, with their age-specific transfer coefficients. Those tables print three figures; where Publication 134 or 137 prints a rate with four, the four are used (iodide to the urinary bladder at 11.84 d−1, uranium at 15.43, barium to the adult’s colon at 20.16, and others, in seventeen elements), at every age where the rate is the same or a plain multiple of the adult’s. The ICRP’s coefficients are those of the four figures: with 11.8, every dose of every iodine isotope comes out 0.2 to 0.3 % above the annex’s. The adult’s values apply from 20 years, from 25 years for the alkaline earths, lead and the actinides (Publication 158 para 13) and, as the ICRP’s own coefficients show, for yttrium, zirconium, niobium, hafnium, tantalum, francium, aluminium and the lanthanides (the actinides being actinium to fermium). In a chain every member has its adult values from the adult age of the element taken in: niobium formed from 93Mo from 20 years (with niobium’s own 25, the bone surface dose after an intake at 15 years or by an adult comes out 13–14 % below the annex’s), and from that age the bone turnover at which progeny leave bone volume is the adult’s too (after ingested 214Bi, bismuth being adult from 20 years, the bone surface dose at 15 years and for adults comes out 1 % low with turnover adult from 25, and skin and gonads 2–3 % high at 15 years). For gadolinium the adult’s liver takes 15 % of what leaves the circulation, as the draft’s table of parameters and Publication 141 give it; the draft’s table of transfer coefficients prints europium’s 25 % there. Mercury vapour absorbed from the lungs enters blood by the way it got there: dissolved as vapour to Plasma 0, the rest as Hg2+ to Plasma 1 (Part 3 draft paras 367–368).
    • Dosimetry: the specific absorbed fractions of Publications 133 and 155 for the twelve reference individuals (newborn, 1, 5, 10 and 15 years, adult; male and female), with the radiations of Publication 107 (or of the decay data chosen), interpolated in energy and in age as Publication 158 prescribes (PCHIP; a weighted linear interpolation in the first year). In age each kind of radiation is interpolated by itself, photons, electrons, alpha particles and neutrons: the spline of a sum is not the sum of the splines, and from bone and marrow to the bone surface the photons’ absorbed fractions rise from the newborn to the 1-year-old where the electrons’ fall (with one spline for their sum, a 1-year-old’s bone surface dose comes out 5 % below the annex’s after 133Ba and 5 % above it after 91Nb). A beta spectrum gives a target the integral of energy × spectrum × absorbed fraction, taken as the cubic (PCHIP) through that product at the spectrum’s tabulated energies; summed there by the trapezoidal rule, tritium comes out 0.15 % and 92Y 0.3 % above the annex’s, and the nose and throat after inhaled tritium particles, which only the top of the spectrum reaches, 1 to 1.5 % below. All this with 1 MeV taken as 1.6 × 10−13 J, the rounded factor the ICRP’s coefficients carry (with the exact 1.602 × 10−13 every dose would be 0.136 % higher than theirs).
    • Effective dose: the tissue weighting factors of Publication 103 applied to the average of the male and female equivalent doses, the remainder the arithmetic mean of its thirteen tissues.

    Decay data

    A dose coefficient rests on decay data: each nuclide’s half-life, the daughters it decays to and the radiations it emits per decay, the energies and yields of its alpha particles, beta spectra, gamma and X-rays, conversion and Auger electrons. Each system has its own, and the ICRP’s coefficients are calculated with them: Publication 38 (1983) in the ICRP 60 system, Publication 107 (2008) in the ICRP 103 system. The Decay data setting, and the same choice on the Batch and Radon at home tabs, puts in their place decay data made on this site from a release of ENSDF, the Evaluated Nuclear Structure Data File of the National Nuclear Data Center (the release the Chart of Nuclides shows). The models of the body stay those of the system.

    Any other release can be added. Open ENSDF… reads a release zip as NNDC publishes it (or the parts of an older one, or the ensdf.001 … ensdf.300 files out of one) from your computer, and the page makes its decay data in your browser, in some ten seconds for a whole release on a desktop computer; nothing is uploaded. The release is then kept in this browser, offered in the Decay data menus here and as a database on the Chart of Nuclides and Radionuclide Decay Chains, until you choose Forget; a release opened on those pages is offered here too, and made the first time it is chosen. The menus also list the releases of NNDC’s archive and say where to download each: NNDC’s server does not let other sites read its files. Files can be dropped on the page as well.

    Publication 107 was itself made from ENSDF: its authors ran the program EDISTR04 over the decay data sets of 2004 (JAERI 1347). The decay data here are made in the same way, by the same program whether on this site or in your browser:

    • From each decay data set: the gamma rays, conversion electrons, alpha groups and beta and capture branches, per decay as its normalisation record says. Where a data set gives no feeding of its levels, each level’s feeding comes from the balance of its gamma rays.
    • Computed: beta spectra and mean energies from Fermi theory with screening and the finite size of the nucleus, as EDISTR04 computed them; the shares of positrons and capture from their rate functions where a branch gives only its total; conversion coefficients by shell, the evaluators’ own where they give them and otherwise the theory EDISTR04 used; X-rays and Auger electrons by following each vacancy through the atomic relaxation data of EPICS2017; spontaneous fission from the formulas and parameters of Publication 107.
    • Checked: run on the decay data sets Publication 107 was made from, the same program reproduces it. Of 2676 dose coefficients (every ICRP 103 nuclide by each route, at six ages), 2582 agree within 0.1 % and 2658 within 1 % at every age.
    • Names: a nuclide keeps the name its system gives it. ENSDF orders some states otherwise (its 2026 release calls the 2.36 h state of tantalum-178 the ground state, which Publication 107 calls Ta-178m), so the states of each nuclide are paired by half-life, and the Decay chain tab gives ENSDF’s name beside where it differs. ENSDF has nuclides the ICRP’s data lack; the ICRP 103 list grows by them.
    • The difference it makes: with ENSDF 2026, nine in ten of the ICRP 103 coefficients stay within 5 % of those with Publication 107 at every age. The large differences are evaluations made since 2004, such as the alpha branch of Pu-232, the gamma-ray intensities of Yb-175 and Ta-177 and the capture of Lu-165 to high-lying levels. Where a data set is incomplete or contradicts itself, the page follows rules of its own (an NR that the data set’s own balance contradicts, levels fed but given no gamma ray, a mode given only as an upper limit), and says so on the Decay chain tab.
    • Not followed: double beta decay, whose half-lives are of 1018 years and more; fission neutrons count only for the 28 nuclides for which Publication 107 gives neutron absorbed fractions; an isomer of less than a minute that decays by IT is part of the decay that makes it.

    Progeny

    A radionuclide’s dose coefficient includes the dose from its radioactive progeny formed in the body after the intake. How the progeny move is where the two systems, and this page, need most care.

    In the ICRP 60 system the progeny follow what DCAL’s batch files for Publication 72 give them: the parent’s model (shared kinetics) for most chains, element-specific models for the chains of lead, radium, thorium and uranium, iodine from tellurium and noble gases (independent kinetics), as Publications 67, 69 and 71 describe. In a chain with independent kinetics the tissues that any member’s model carries it into are the chain’s, and part of each member’s activity in the rest of the body is counted in them by their share of the mass, as DCAL does it (ORNL/TM-2001/190, section 9.3.1): a tissue the parent’s model names takes its share of the member’s own rest of the body where the member’s model leaves it out; a tissue the parent’s model leaves out takes its share of the parent’s rest of the body, where the member was formed, from the first member whose model carries it there and every member after it (from those before, of their own). After an inhalation of Type S material, a member with no f1 of its own for Type S is absorbed from the gut no more than the parent, as Publication 72’s coefficients show (DCAL itself takes the member’s own f1). A chain ends where DCAL ends it; Publication 72 ends a few earlier, before a long-lived member (U-239 and U-240 before plutonium, Th-226 before Pb-210), so that its doses to the liver, bone and gonads for those are lower than this page’s and DCAL’s, its effective doses up to 4 % lower.

    In the ICRP 103 system Publication 158 (section 2.5) gives the general rules: in the respiratory tract every member keeps the parent’s absorption parameters, but noble gases escape at 100 d−1; progeny formed in the alimentary tract are absorbed with their own reference fA (after an inhalation of particles, times the parent’s fr; after a gas or vapour, their own fA alone, as the ICRP’s coefficients for ruthenium tetroxide have it), which for an element with a higher value in food is that of its other soluble forms, as the ICRP’s coefficients have it: antimony formed from 124nSb is absorbed with 0.05 and not with the 0.1 of antimony in food (with 0.1 the doses to the brain and the thyroid after ingestion come out 1.5 to 1.75 times the annex’s); isotopes of the parent’s element share its model. The ground state formed from an isomer that was taken in is the exception: where the parent’s section gives its element a model as a progeny, it has that model, and the parent’s soft-tissue pools are no more its own than any progeny’s. 235U formed from 235mU has the skin, spleen, marrow and gonads of uranium as a progeny (on the parent’s kinetics the female’s skin dose comes out 6 % below the ICRP’s for workers), and with 242Am formed from 242mAm kept in the parent’s pools, skin and the tissues of the rest of the body come out 3 % above the ICRP’s for workers. How a progeny of another element moves once it is formed in the body is set by the parent element’s section of the OIR series (Publications 134, 137, 141 and 151, “Treatment of radioactive progeny”), the same for every chain that element heads, and this page carries those sections’ models:

    • chains of lead, bismuth and polonium (Publication 137 section 9.2.3.3): lead and bismuth with red marrow, spleen, skin, testes and ovaries added to their models, polonium entering blood at Plasma 2, and models of their own for thallium, gold and mercury. Thallium formed in lead’s red cells stays in red cells: only thallium formed in a part of the blood that the thallium model does not have goes to plasma (para 481). The annex’s coefficients for 194Pb to 200Pb need it (with that thallium sent to plasma they come out up to 7.5 % lower). Mercury follows mercury’s own model, the Part 3 draft’s for divalent mercury, without its brain compartments and with red marrow, skin, testes and ovaries taking the shares that para 479 gives them; gold formed in mercury’s kidneys or liver is in its own. The annex’s 195mPb and 197mPb show it: with para 479’s model throughout, the kidney doses after inhaling them as Type F come out 7 to 14 % higher, and liver and spleen up to 9 %. Lead, bismuth, polonium or thallium formed in the soft-tissue pools of a member that is itself a progeny is in its own pools of that name, as in radium’s chains: after ingested 214Bi or 214Pb, whose dose to soft tissue comes from the 210Pb they leave, the doses to the testes come out 6 to 13 % lower with the 210Bi formed in that lead’s pools sent to blood;
    • radon and thoron (section 12.3.3.3): lead, bismuth, polonium and thallium as in lead’s chains, with the two fat and the two breast compartments of radon’s model added to theirs; formed in the air of the lungs a progeny is exhaled, formed in radon’s blood it goes to its own plasma. Every other compartment of radon’s model has its counterpart in theirs: formed in radon’s liver or kidneys they are in their own liver and kidneys, and formed in the yellow marrow, which Publication 158 added to radon’s model, they leave it as they leave fat, at the rate of their intermediate soft tissue. The annex’s coefficients show both: sent to blood at the rate of the fast soft-tissue pool instead, the liver dose after radon gas comes out 13 % low for infants and 13 % high for adults, the kidneys’ up to 38 % high and red marrow’s up to 22 % low. The progeny’s uptake to breast is the section’s for adults and follows the breast’s share of the blood flow at other ages, as radon’s model has it (with the adult’s uptake at every age, an infant’s breast dose after thoron comes out 12 % high). What radon’s progeny form in the stomach moves on with its contents at radon’s own rate, slower in the female from 15 years (at the tract’s usual rate her stomach dose after swallowed radon comes out 13 % low);
    • chains of radium, thorium and uranium (sections 13.2.3.3, 14.2.3.3 and 15.3.3): the lead-chain models with trabecular and cortical marrow; radium, thorium, uranium, actinium and neptunium with added spleen, skin, marrow and gonads; protactinium with thorium’s model; francium and astatine decay where they are formed. Thorium’s spleen and skin take 0.5 % and 2 % of what leaves the adult’s circulation (para 715), and at other ages the same part of what its intermediate soft tissue takes (as fixed fractions at every age the spleen and skin doses of 228Ra come out up to 46 % above the annex’s in children). Actinium’s skin and spleen take 0.13 and 0.043 d−1 from blood, para 714’s shares of its soft-tissue deposition to two figures, as the annex’s 225Ra has them. Radium formed in thorium’s bone volume leaves as from non-exchangeable bone (para 812) and stays in the compartment it was formed in, so that the actinium and thorium it decays to are in their own bone volume and pass the marrow on their way out (joined to radium’s own bone instead, the red marrow dose of 232Th comes out 4.5 % lower). Plutonium formed after uranium (239U, 240U) follows the model Publication 137 para 921 gives it there, with 30 % of what leaves blood going to a soft-tissue pool that returns it and 60 % of the rest to the liver, not plutonium’s own (with that, the liver dose after 240U comes out 7 % below the ICRP’s for workers);
    • the other actinides (Publication 141 section 18.2.10): each element’s model with skin and spleen added, which empty as its intermediate soft tissue does, plutonium without its fast blood compartment. Americium formed in plutonium’s slowest liver compartment, which no other actinide’s model has, stays in the liver, in its own slow compartment, as the ICRP’s coefficients for 241Pu have it (sent to blood, as from a compartment the model lacks, the liver dose comes out 12 % lower);
    • caesium, barium, the lanthanides, tellurium, antimony, iodine, strontium, yttrium, zirconium, niobium, molybdenum, ruthenium, iridium, iron, cobalt, zinc, calcium and sulphur: their sections’ models, among them those for scandium, manganese, gallium, rubidium, rhodium, osmium, platinum and rhenium as progeny, and the model of 137mBa formed from 137Cs (with Publication 158 para 450 for children). That barium has a pool in the extracellular fluid of each soft tissue of caesium’s model (Publication 137 para 317), adipose tissue among them with its 5 % of the blood flow, though the paragraph’s list leaves it out (with adipose tissue counted in the other soft tissue, the doses to breast, salivary glands and gonads after 137Cs come out 1–2 % below the annex’s). A lanthanide formed from another follows its own model; all of them share one structure, the soft-tissue pools included (Publication 141 para 86). Barium formed on a lanthanide’s bone surfaces is on barium’s own, and caesium formed on barium’s leaves them at caesium’s own rate, as in barium’s chains (with both sent to blood at once, the bone surface dose of 129La comes out 19 % below the ICRP’s for workers). Lanthanum formed on barium’s bone surfaces is on its own bone surfaces, and only lanthanum formed in barium’s bone volume leaves at the bone turnover rate (Publication 137 paras 354 and 356); cerium formed from lanthanum follows the same model, its soft-tissue pools included. The annex’s coefficients need both: without them 140Ba comes out 2.8 % lower at 3 months, and the liver doses of 141Ba up to 5 % lower. Iodine formed from tellurium or antimony has one compartment for inorganic iodide in other tissue where iodine’s own model has two (Publication 137 paras 175 and 135); with the two, 134Te comes out 4 % higher and 132Te 2 % higher. Gallium formed in zinc’s kidneys is in gallium’s kidneys and leaves them for the bladder, with the half-time Publication 134 para 437 gives (with that gallium sent to blood, 72Zn comes out 2–3 % higher); before adulthood it takes half as much again to the bone surfaces, as zinc’s own model and gallium’s in the Part 3 draft do (with the adult’s rates at every age, the red marrow and bone surface doses of 72Zn come out 5–10 % lower in children). Rubidium formed from strontium follows rubidium’s own model, with kidneys and liver, where the condensed model of Publication 134 para 484 has them in other tissue (with that model the kidney doses of 80Sr and 82Sr come out at a quarter to a half); strontium’s bone surfaces are rubidium’s. Scandium formed from calcium follows scandium’s own model by age, the Part 3 draft’s (with the adult’s model of Publication 134 at every age, the doses to liver, kidneys and spleen of 47Ca come out 3–4 % lower at 3 months). Antimony formed in tellurium’s red cells is in antimony’s red cells: Publication 137 para 177 calls only tellurium’s liver and other soft tissue ambiguous (sent to plasma, the bladder dose of 118Te comes out 10–19 % higher);
    • nickel, selenium and silver (Publication 151 sections 15, 20 and 25): cobalt formed from nickel; arsenic, bromine and krypton from selenium; rhodium, palladium, cadmium and indium from silver, with the adult characteristic models of Publication 151 at every age (which reproduces the annex of Publication 158), extended by the parent’s tissues as its Annex B describes. Cobalt formed in nickel’s two kidney compartments is in cobalt’s own, the urinary path and the other kidney tissue (sent to blood, the kidney dose of 56Ni comes out 3–4 % higher);
    • the elements of Part 3 (Publication 151, the section of each): magnesium, silicon, chlorine, potassium, titanium, chromium, manganese, gallium, germanium, arsenic, bromine, rubidium, rhodium, palladium, cadmium, indium, tin, hafnium, tantalum, tungsten, rhenium, osmium, platinum, gold, mercury, thallium, astatine and francium as parents, each progeny with its own model and the tissues and rates the section adds; argon formed from chlorine leaves tissue with a half-time of 15 minutes. Polonium formed from astatine enters blood at Plasma 1, not at Plasma 2 as in lead chains: the Part 3 draft’s 210At coefficients need it (with Plasma 2 they come out 7–12 % lower).

    A progeny formed in a compartment that its model also has (the same tissue, of the same name) is in that compartment, but the unspecific soft-tissue pools (ST0–ST2) of another element’s model are not its own, except in the chains of lead, radium, thorium, uranium and the other actinides: there a member formed in the pools of another progeny’s model takes its own pool of the same name, though not in those of the chain’s parent. The ICRP’s coefficients show both: with a progeny’s pools foreign, the kidney doses of 225Ra and 228Ra come out 6 to 13 % above the annex’s, and the tissues of the rest of the body after 229Th, 241Np or 249Cm 20 to 45 % below the ICRP’s for workers; with the parent’s pools the members’ own, 229Th and 232Th come out 12 % higher. Thorium formed in radium’s own bone volume, exchangeable or not, returns to blood at the bone turnover rate. Formed anywhere else it moves to its own blood at the rate the section gives: 1000 d−1 from blood, a rate of its own model from soft tissue, the bone turnover rate from bone volume. While a progeny is in its parent’s bone volume, that bone volume is one of its own source regions, as the ICRP’s coefficients have it: the rest of the body, over which it spreads its activity in tissues its model does not name, leaves the bone volume out (caesium’s model has none, and for caesium formed from barium the rest of the body would otherwise be mostly bone). As in the ICRP 60 system, the tissues that any member’s model names are the chain’s: of a member’s activity in the rest of the body, each such tissue that its own model leaves out takes its share by mass, with its own dose per transformation, and only what is left is spread over the remaining tissues. That is so for the tissues that the members after it name. A tissue that only the members before it name is no part of its rest of the body: it holds what is formed there, and nothing of what the member’s own model spreads from blood (Publication 151 describes its progeny models so: the element’s model with compartments added for the tissues of the models it is linked to). The ICRP’s coefficients show it. Arsenic’s model has no pancreas, spleen or gonads and selenium’s has: with arsenic’s rest of the body spread over them too, their doses after 70Se and 72Se come out 4 to 7 % above the annex’s. And after 129La, whose barium and caesium lack lanthanum’s liver, kidneys, marrow and gonads, the doses to salivary glands, thymus and breast come out 11 to 16 % below the ICRP’s for workers and the testes’ 9 % above. The shares are the reference male’s at every age, for both sexes, the masses straight in age between the phantoms’; an organ only the female has takes her mass over his rest of the body. Radon has a model for each sex: after thoron the female’s shares are the male’s as everywhere, after 222Rn they are by her own masses, as the annex has each (her skin, spleen and ovaries differ by 5 to 25 % between the two); no rule that tells the two apart was found. The ICRP’s coefficients show the shares: 210mBi, whose only progeny 206Tl names skin, spleen, red marrow and gonads, comes out in the adult female 16 % low in skin and 21 % high in ovaries by her own masses, and in the 10-year-old male 41 % low in testes with one dose per transformation for the whole rest of the body; with the shares every tissue agrees at every age in both sexes, and so do the chains whose progeny name muscle (82Sr, 113Ag, 129Ba). Thallium, bismuth and polonium as progeny in lead’s chains name bone volume whether or not the chain puts anything there (with the mineral bone in the rest of the body of the polonium formed from 210Bi, the tissues its model does not name come out 5 to 8 % lower, by the bone’s share of the mass at each age). A model with the marrow of trabecular and cortical bone has all the marrow there, so that its rest of the body has none (counted twice, the tissues outside actinium’s named ones after 225Ra come out up to 5 % lower). Where a chain has such a model, the marrow’s share of another member’s rest of the body is in those two regions and not in red and yellow marrow: the mass is the same, divided otherwise, and the dose per transformation to the bone surface and the tissues near bone differs (as red and yellow marrow, the bone surface dose after 238U, whose thorium has that marrow, comes out 3 % below the ICRP’s for workers, and a 10-year-old’s thymus dose after 72Zn 2 % below the annex’s). And the tissues that count as the chain’s are those of the members the ICRP’s calculations carry, which this page takes as those that emit a thousandth of the chain’s energy over 50 years, with the alpha energy weighted by 20 or without, and what leads to those, where a thousandth of them comes that way: the 218At on a branch of 0.02 % of 218Po is none (with astatine’s thyroid counted, the members after it leave it out of their rest of the body, and a 3-month-old’s thyroid dose after radon comes out 10 % low), nor is the 230Th that grows in from 234U, the 234U from 238Pu or the 239Pu from 243Cm. Such a member is followed if the cut-off keeps it, but the tissues its model names take no share (with those of the last three counted, the adult female’s skin dose comes out 20 % higher). Noble gases formed in the body follow the OIR series’ generic model: to blood at 100 d−1 from bone surface, 1.5 or 0.36 d−1 from bone volume, with a half-time of 30, 20 or 15 minutes from soft tissue (radon, xenon, krypton), exhaled at 1000 d−1. What such a gas decays to while it is in blood is in its own blood at once, as the ICRP’s coefficients for 226Ra have it: polonium entering blood is in a plasma compartment a fifth of which goes straight to the kidneys, and moved there at 1000 d−1, as from a blood compartment its model lacks, a quarter of the 218Po (3 minutes) decays on the way and the kidney dose comes out up to 4 % low. Activity secreted into the alimentary tract is reabsorbed with the element’s highest reference fA, for polonium 0.1, the value the ICRP’s own calculations used. Where a section changes a model by shares of its flows (the spleen’s share of the soft tissue, say), the same shares are applied to the children’s models.

    A progeny that its parent’s section does not name (yttrium from zirconium, say) falls back to simpler rules: in a compartment its model also has, it is there; formed in bone volume it leaves at the parent’s rates; elsewhere it returns to its blood at the rate of its own model’s intermediate soft tissue.

    When progeny of other elements emit a tenth or more of a chain’s energy, the Coefficients tab says so: those results rest on these rules, and can differ from the ICRP’s by more than its rounding (see below).

    How the calculation is done

    Each intake becomes one system of linear differential equations: the activity of every chain member in every compartment, with transfer rates interpolated linearly in age between the reference ages (the rates change as the child grows), and decay feeding each member’s progeny. The equivalent dose to each target region is the activity in each source region times its dose per transformation (SEE in the ICRP 60 system, S in the ICRP 103 system), integrated over the commitment period; the dose per transformation also changes with age. In the ICRP 60 system it goes from one phantom to the next by the weights DCAL gives the older phantom, read off DCAL’s own dose rates: t0.3 + 0.7 (1 − t)10 in the first year (t in years), x0.5 + 0.5 (1 − x)5 between 1 and 5 years (x the fraction of the interval) and the age itself from 5 years on; in a chain with independent kinetics the shares of a member’s activity in the rest of the body that count in other tissues change linearly in age, as DCAL has them, so that the dose there is a product of the two. In the ICRP 103 system it follows a monotone cubic spline (PCHIP) through the six reference ages, with a weighted interpolation in the first year that follows the growth curves of Publication 89. Between two reference ages the dose per transformation is therefore one polynomial in the weights for every source and target, and in the ICRP 60 system the integrator carries, for each group of compartments that share a source region, the integral of their activity weighted by each of its basis functions (two, or four for the products): the doses to all targets follow from these exactly, at the end of each stretch.

    The ICRP 103 system moves in steps, as the calculation behind the ICRP’s coefficients does: from the intake on, the transfer rates and the dose per transformation are held over steps of a fortieth of a year (about nine days), each at the value of the age the step starts at. Publication 158 describes both as changing continuously with age (para 13 and eqs. 2.15–2.16), but its coefficients are those of the steps: for nuclides whose model does not change with age (silver, cobalt, iridium, antimony, tellurium and others, by injection), a continuous calculation is low for 3-month-olds by an amount that grows with the half-life — 0.1 % at 6 hours, 0.3 % at 17 hours, 1 % at 3 days, 1.2 to 1.8 % from 5 days on — by 0.2 to 0.35 % for 1-year-olds and 0.1 % for 5-year-olds, which is what holding the values for the first days and following them half a step late afterwards gives; the step that closes the gap is 9.0 to 9.1 days at each of the three ages. Three elements are the exception: the ICRP’s coefficients for calcium and polonium taken in before the age of 1 year, and for zinc before 5 years, are those of steps of one day, and so they are calculated here (the step that reproduces them is 1.0 to 1.5 days for 3-month-olds and 1.1 to 1.3 for zinc at 1 year, against 8.9 to 9.3 days for 23 other elements; in the nine-day steps their 3-month-olds come out 0.7 to 1.4 % above the annex’s). No rule was found that tells these cases from the others. Where the rates change with age they are held too: 90Sr at 3 months is 1.3 % low with both continuous, 0.8 % with the dose per transformation in steps alone, 0.05 % with both in steps. The integrator then carries each group’s number of transformations, and a dose grows over a step by the step’s dose per transformation times the transformations in it, exactly. The rates are held step by step through the first year of life and for the first three steps of any intake; after that they follow the same straight lines of age half a step late, which is within 0.02 % of holding them all the way and saves restarting the integrator every nine days of childhood. The integrator is a variable-order backward differentiation method (NDF) with the exact sparse Jacobian, restarted wherever a rate table has a corner; it crosses the commitment period in one to two thousand steps however stiff the system (rates from 10−5 to 4·104 d−1) and however long the chain (a few hundred compartments for the actinide series). Specific absorbed fractions are interpolated in energy as each publication says (linearly for the ICRP 60 photon SAFs, as DCAL; PCHIP for the ICRP 103 SAFs), beta particles enter through their spectra.

    The integrals keep the system small: for 226Ra by ingestion, 861 equations with 2,765 coefficients. The ages at intake are separate integrations and run in parallel, each in a worker of its own, as many at a time as the device has cores to spare; so do the inhalations behind radon at home. Before a run, the foot of the settings sums up the model Calculate will solve — the nuclides the cut-off keeps, the compartments, transfers and equations, and the number of integrations — and the Model and Decay chain tabs show it. Results stay on their tabs when a setting is changed, marked as not of the current settings until Calculate runs again.

    The Model tab draws each systemic model of the ICRP 103 system as the ICRP’s figure of it stands (Publication 158 and the drafts of Parts 2 and 3; a progeny’s model as the figure of its own element), with the parts of the alimentary tract and anything else the figure does not show beside it and below; arrows from outside the boxes name where activity comes in. A model without a figure of its own takes the figure of a model with nearly the same compartments, where its arrows cross no more boxes than in a layout made for it; any other, among them those of the ICRP 60 system, is laid out as the ICRP’s figures are — the blood down the middle, soft tissue at the top, bone on the left, the liver and the gut on the right — or, where the tissues empty into the excreta rather than back into the blood, with the blood on the left, the tissues beside it and the bladder and the gut on the right; boxes that may stand on either side stand where the arrows cross the fewest other boxes. The arrows go round the boxes, as the figures draw them.

    After a calculation the Model tab can show where the activity is and where the dose comes from. Under Show, Activity in the body fills each box of the drawing, as a bucket, to its share of the chosen member’s activity in the whole body at a time after intake, and Effective dose to its share of the committed effective dose received up to that time, or of its rate then, from the activity in the box: the member’s, or the whole chain’s. The time is one of the calculation’s output times, ten to a decade from about a minute and a half after intake, for one of its ages at intake; Run through moves it to the last. Each compartment’s dose is its own, not a share of its source region’s: with a time series the integrator keeps the integrals of every compartment apart (otherwise those of a member in a source region together), which takes a tenth to a quarter longer. The drawing has one box for each part of the gut’s contents and for the bladder’s, whichever copy of the alimentary tract the activity is in; with the whole chain, a nuclide counts in the box of the same name, or, where it was formed in a compartment of another member’s model, in that compartment’s box. What the drawing has no box for — the respiratory tract, the mouth and the oesophagus, and the compartments a progeny is given where it is formed in a part of another member’s model that its own model lacks — is listed under the slider with its share.

    Batch calculations

    The Batch tab calculates many coefficients at once, for one system and one route: the radionuclides typed or pasted into its field (names such as Cs-137 or cs137, separated by commas, spaces or lines; an element’s symbol stands for all its nuclides in the system), or chosen from the list that Choose from the list opens — the system’s radionuclides to the left, narrowed by a search, an element, a half-life range, a decay mode and whether the route has forms for them, and the chosen ones to the right, moved with the buttons (the selected ones, or all that are shown), by double-clicking or with Enter. Each is calculated in all its chemical and physical forms for the route or in its default one, for inhalation at each aerosol size ticked (a gas or vapour has no aerosol size and is calculated once), at each age at intake ticked, with the tab’s own decay chain cut-off (ICRP 103 system) and tolerance. By the route External each is calculated instead in each geometry ticked, at each age (the adult only in the ICRP 60 system), all its ages in one request of a few milliseconds; its table holds dose rates per Bq m−3, or per Bq m−2 on the ground as its Per column says, and if chosen FGR 12’s HE and every quantity with the progeny in equilibrium. The count above the button says how many calculations that is. They run in the page’s workers, as many at a time as the device has cores to spare, after any single calculation started meanwhile; the bar shows how far the batch has come and about how long is left, and Stop ends it, keeping what is done. A calculation that fails is noted in its row and the batch goes on.

    Under the bar is what the table shows, which calculates nothing again when changed: the committed effective dose, and if chosen the committed equivalent doses of the tissues weighted in it, of the remainder’s tissues and of the others, in the ICRP 103 system for each sex as well; a row for each radionuclide, form and aerosol size with the ages as columns, or a row for each age as well; and the digits. Save as CSV and Save as Excel write the whole table, six significant figures in CSV and the full values in Excel, the workbook with a second sheet of the settings.

    Risk coefficients

    The Risk tab recalculates the ICRP’s detriment-adjusted nominal risk coefficients from the inputs its recommendations give, tissue by tissue, beside the values it printed:

    • ICRP 103 system (Publication 103 Table 1 and Annex A): for each tissue the nominal risk R, lifetime cases per 10 000 persons per Sv, is adjusted for lethality k and for the quality of life of those who survive, R (k + q (1 − k)) with q = qmin + (1 − qmin) k, and weighted by the relative cancer-free life lost l. Summed, this gives 5.7 × 10−2 Sv−1 for the whole population and 4.2 × 10−2 Sv−1 for adults; Table 1 lists the heritable part before the life-lost weighting (para A 164).
    • ICRP 60 system (Publication 60 Table 3 and Annex B): for each organ the probability of fatal cancer F, with non-fatal cancers weighted by their lethality, F (2 − k), times the relative length of life lost l / l̄, plus severe hereditary effects: 7.3 × 10−2 Sv−1 for the whole population and 5.6 × 10−2 Sv−1 for workers, the basis of its tissue weighting factors.

    The nominal risks themselves come from risk models fitted to the atomic bomb survivors and carried to other populations with life tables and age weights that the publications do not give, so the calculation starts from them; the tab shows for each tissue where its value comes from (the mix of excess relative and excess absolute risk models, or another study) and the tissue weighting factor its relative detriment led to.

    Once a dose coefficient is calculated, the tab also gives its nominal detriment per becquerel (for external exposure, per second per unit concentration), two ways: the committed effective dose times the total coefficient (5.7 × 10−2 Sv−1, or 7.3 in the ICRP 60 system), and tissue by tissue, each tissue’s detriment per sievert times its committed equivalent dose. They agree when the dose is spread through the body and part when it gathers in a few tissues (iodine in the thyroid gives about half), because the weighting factors are rounded and grouped detriments. Both are nominal values for protection purposes: the coefficients are averages over the sexes and the ages at exposure, and the ICRP does not recommend them, or effective dose, for the risk of an individual (Publication 103 paras 155–161, A 164).

    Radon and thoron at home

    For radon (222Rn) and thoron (220Rn) indoors the ICRP gives doses per exposure, not per becquerel taken in: most of the dose comes from the short-lived progeny the air carries, as tiny unattached clusters or attached to the aerosol, and is reckoned from their potential alpha energy concentration times the time spent in it (mJ h m−3; 1 WLM = 3.54 mJ h m−3), or from the gas concentration through the equilibrium factor F. The Radon at home tab calculates them as Publication 158 does (Section 32, Annex C), with the method of Publication 137 (Annex A):

    • each progeny nuclide (218Po, 214Pb, 214Bi; 212Pb, 212Bi) in each mode of the home aerosol — unattached, nucleation and accumulation — is an inhalation calculated by this page’s engine, with the regional deposition of Table C.1, the absorption of Table 32.1 and the element’s systemic model;
    • per mode they are combined per unit potential alpha energy with the progeny’s activity ratios and the breathing rate at home of each age (Table 32.3), and the modes weighted by the unattached fraction fp and the nucleation share fpn (Table 32.2), which you can change;
    • the gas itself adds its own dose per exposure, from the radon model (Annex C para C 18).

    Against Publication 158 the radon progeny agree within 1 % at every age (Table 32.7), the gas within 2 % (Table C.7) and each nuclide and mode within the rounding of Table C.8. Thoron progeny come out 2–5 % higher than Table 32.8: Table C.1 gives the deposition of the 200 nm accumulation mode for radon progeny’s spread (σg 2.0), not for thoron progeny’s narrower one (1.8). The tab also shows the coefficients the ICRP recommends for every age, 3 mSv per mJ h m−3 for radon and 1 for thoron (paras 532–537), and an annual dose for a concentration and the hours spent at home.

    External exposure

    The route External gives dose rate coefficients for a person outside the activity: in a cloud of contaminated air, in contaminated water, standing on contaminated ground or over contaminated soil. Nothing is taken into the body. The coefficient is the equivalent dose rate to each tissue, and the effective dose rate, per unit concentration around the body: Sv s−1 per Bq m−3 of air, water or soil, or per Bq m−2 of ground (per hour or per year if chosen), as the US Environmental Protection Agency’s Federal Guidance Reports give them: Report 15 (2019, revised in July 2025) in the ICRP 103 system, Report 12 (1993) in the ICRP 60 system. The page does not copy the reports’ table of each nuclide. It adds up each nuclide’s radiations with the reports’ own doses per photon and per electron of a single energy, as the reports do, so that every radionuclide of the decay data is covered (838 of Publication 38, 1252 of Publication 107, more with ENSDF), noble gases and nuclides of seconds among them:

    • Photons: the reports’ monoenergetic coefficients. FGR 15 gives 29 tissues of the stylised phantoms of Han et al. at six ages (newborn, 1, 5, 10 and 15 years, and the reference adult, which takes the adult phantom for the male and the 15-year-old’s for the female) at 13 energies from 10 keV to 5 MeV; FGR 12 gives 25 organs of the Cristy–Eckerman adult hermaphrodite at 12 energies. Each photon line of 10 keV or more counts at its yield, the coefficient interpolated in the logarithms of energy and dose rate by a monotone cubic (PCHIP; the cubic of the last interval above 5 MeV), FGR 15’s zeros taken as 10−100 as its authors took them.
    • Bremsstrahlung: beta particles slowing down in the medium (soil for the ground) give off photons whose spectrum the reports’ Appendix C builds from the scaled spectra of Seltzer and Berger (its eq. C-2), here interpolated by PCHIP in k/T and in ln T; their dose follows from the same monoenergetic coefficients. It is all a pure beta emitter gives the tissues under the skin.
    • Electrons: their dose to the skin, 70 µm deep, from FGR 12’s DOSFACTER curves (its Figs. II.24–II.26, which FGR 15 uses too), over the beta spectrum, taken as a PCHIP of its tabulated values, and the conversion and Auger electrons. From the ground they are counted at 1 m and must have 0.37 MeV to get there; FGR 15 starts them at the surface, its photons 3 mm deep (0.5 g cm−2, for the ground’s roughness). FGR 12’s ground is a smooth plane.
    • Effective dose: in the ICRP 103 system, Publication 103’s tissue weighting factors on the hermaphrodite phantoms, as FGR 15 applies them (gonads the mean of testes and ovaries, prostate/uterus the mean of the two, the remainder the mean of its 13 tissues, the skin with its electrons). In the ICRP 60 system, Publication 60’s weighting factors with the remainder of Publication 72, mass-weighted with the splitting rule, without the extrathoracic airways, which FGR 12’s phantom does not have; and beside it the quantity FGR 12 tabulates, the effective dose equivalent HE of ICRP 26 (gonads 0.25, breast 0.15, red marrow and lung 0.12, thyroid and bone surface 0.03, the five highest of the other organs 0.06 each, the skin left out). FGR 12 gives the effective dose of ICRP 60 too, for photons of single energies only (its Tables II.4–II.15): this weighting reproduces it within 0.7 % at every energy and geometry.
    • Progeny: the reports’ coefficients are of the nuclide alone, and so is the page’s first column. Beside it is the nuclide with its progeny in equilibrium: each member that lives less long than the parent and is formed only from members that do, at its activity per becquerel of the parent once the chain is in equilibrium — the branching fraction in secular equilibrium, more where the parent’s half-life is not much longer (transient equilibrium, λi/(λi − λP) at each step: 1.15 Bq of La-140 per Bq of Ba-140). The Decay chain tab lists these activities, each member’s own coefficient and its share, and how long the chain takes to come within 1 % of equilibrium: 17 minutes for Cs-137 and Ba-137m, 1.6 million years for the whole U-238 series. A member that lives as long as its parent or longer never comes into equilibrium with it (Am-241 from Pu-241, Pb-210 from Rn-222): its dose rate depends on how long the parent has been there, and its own coefficient is the one to add.
    • Not included, as in the reports: the neutrons of spontaneous fission; shielding by buildings, clothes or the ground’s unevenness; the embryo and fetus.

    What it does not do

    • Intakes by the embryo and fetus, and by infants through breast milk (Publications 88 and 95).
    • Wounds as such (the NCRP wound model), and occupational exposure (the OIR series for workers). Direct uptake to blood is calculated in the ICRP 103 system (route Injection).
    • Radon and thoron in workplaces, mines and caves (Publication 137), and actinon (219Rn); homes are on the Radon at home tab.
    • Argon, krypton and xenon as intakes: the ICRP treats them as external exposure to the gas around the body, which the route External gives.
