Reactive transport in the browser

Write a reaction set the way you would write it down, say whether it sits in a stirred batch or in a column with diffusion and advection, and this page builds the equations, differentiates them into an analytic sparse Jacobian and integrates them with the same stiff solvers as the canister radiolysis page. Nothing is uploaded: the whole thing runs here.

The button at the right end of the tabs gives the page the whole window, without the site's header and footer; the kvot mark at the left of the tabs then goes to the home page, and the switch beside the button changes between light and dark. The page remembers the choice.

The tabs follow the work: the model, then the run, then the chemistry of its waters, then the tabs that run the model many times, then the solver's check. On a tab that draws the run, the bar at the top chooses what is drawn, with Export and the tab's (i) at its right end, and the row under it, in smaller type, how it is drawn: the axes, the time and the units. A tab that works something out first — Diagrams, PHREEQC, Sweeps, Fit, Uncertainty — sets it up in a box, with the button that does it at the box's right; what came of it is drawn under the box, with its own bar, once there is something to draw.

The model text

Sixteen sections, of which two are needed: <SPECIES> and <REACTIONS>, and any number of named tables, <TABLE name>. Everything after a # is a comment. A rock matrix beside the column is a few more settings; see A rock matrix. Wherever a number goes it may be written as arithmetic on numbers — 365*86400, 1.33*10**12, 2/3 — and is worked out when the model compiles. Open… loads a model text from a file, and so does dropping the file anywhere on the page; either also reads skbrtm's database files, see Opening skbrtm and BRUM databases.

Colour the syntax, on the Model tab, paints comments, section headings, numbers, function names and the words that mean something where they stand — a setting and the words its value may take, fixed and D on a species line, k and r in a reaction, all and matrix in a parameter. It is a second copy of the text drawn underneath the box you are typing in, which is the only way a plain text area can be coloured while keeping its own undo, selection and caret; turn it off for a very large model, or if the colours are in the way. The choice is remembered between visits.

A name, the concentration it starts at, and then any of: fixed to hold it there for ever, D= its diffusion coefficient in m²/s, and left= / right= the concentration at a Dirichlet face, or what a reservoir there starts with. A species with no D does not diffuse; but in a column with ADVECTION the water carries every species that is not fixed, D or no D. immobile keeps a species where it is whatever the column does, and mineral makes it a pure phase: see Minerals below, and for a mineral that is an end member of a solid solution Solid solutions. exchanger makes it the sites of an ion exchanger and exchange a species on them, with sites= and per= the sites that go with a mineral in its form: see Ion exchange below. surface makes it a species on a surface, with sites= its sites, and potential the surface's potential, with area= and grams=: see Surface complexation below. electron makes it PHREEQC's electron, with pe= its pe: see Redox: the electron below. gas makes it a gas of a gas phase, and Tc=, Pc= and omega= a real one: see A gas phase below. With a rock matrix a species also takes De= (or Dm=), Kd= or Rm=, and Kdf=: see A rock matrix below. With ACTIVITY it takes z=, a= and b=: see Activity below; under ACTIVITY = sit and pitzer, z= alone; under llnl, a=, or drummond for Drummond's coefficient of CO2 (see LLNL).

fixed is a statement about the equation, not a nudge: that species' row in the right-hand side and in the Jacobian is set to exactly zero, so nothing — reaction, diffusion, advection, a Dirichlet face, an equilibrium — can move it. It still drives everything else, and is still consumed on paper by any reaction that consumes it; it simply is not replenished from anywhere the model has to account for. That is what a large water reservoir, a buffered pH or an imposed dose field is. Because the row is zero, the answer for such a species is the number you wrote, and that number is what the charts and the CSV show: a stiff step leaves round-off in it — about one part in 1013 on the built-in model over nine hundred steps — and that is arithmetic rather than chemistry, so it is written back out. If it is ever larger than round-off could explain, the run says so instead of hiding it.

A fixed species inside an <EQUILIBRIUM> is held there too, and the bookkeeping follows: fix the H+ of a water equilibrium and charge stops being conserved, because the buffer supplies whatever the relation calls for. What is left is one relation in one unknown, and OH- goes to K/[H+]. Fixing every species of a relation is refused, since then there is nothing left to satisfy it with.

A name may be a chemical formula: e-, H+, OH-, C2O4-2, UO2+2, U_site, and groups in round brackets, one level deep: Fe(OH)3, UO2(CO3)3-4. Square brackets are what a rate law puts round a name, so a name cannot contain those.

R= (or mass=, the same thing) is the coefficient on the left of the equation rather than the right: R dC/dt = …. A retardation factor for a sorbing species, a porosity, a water content — anything that divides everything happening to that species, transport and reactions alike. R=4 turns diffusion at D into diffusion at D/4 and a decay at k into one at k/4. It may be a plain number or the name of a parameter, in which case it varies from cell to cell. It has to be greater than zero: a zero would make that equation algebraic rather than differential, which is what <EQUILIBRIUM> is for.

<REACTIONS>

A + 2 B => C, k = 1.4E11                       # mass action: k[A][B]²
A <=> B, kf = 1.0, kb = 0.5                     # both ways
S => P, r = vmax*[S]/(km + [S]), vmax = 2, km = 0.1   # a rate law of your own
=> H2O2, k = 1.0E-8                            # nothing on the left is a source
OH- => , k = 0.1                               # nothing on the right is a sink

The equation comes first, then the rate, then any constants the rate uses. With k = you get mass action and the rate is that constant times each reactant raised to its coefficient. With r = you write the rate yourself and a concentration goes in brackets — [S] — so that a name like H+ is never read as arithmetic. A power's exponent may be anything that does not depend on a concentration: a number, arithmetic such as (2/3), a constant of the line or a parameter.

Fe+2 + 0.25 O2 => Fe(OH)3, r = (kr/kh)*[O2]*[Fe+2]*[OH-]^2, kr = 1.33*10**12, kh = 1.3*10**-3
A => B, r = k*c^(2/3)*(1 - [B]/cmax), k = 5e-13, c = [A], cmax = 1.8e-6

A constant may be arithmetic, and may use the constants named before it on its line — kf = 2, kb = kf/4. A constant written c = [A] is an alias: in the law, c is the concentration of A, exactly as if [A] had been written there. Both are how skbrtm writes its databases, and they mean here what they say. (skbrtm itself pastes each constant into the law as text, so it reads (kr/kh) above as 1.33*10**12/1.3*10**-3 — a million times less.)

Calcite => Ca+2 + CO3-2, r = area*(k1*{H+} + k2*{CO2})*(1 - SR(Calcite)), m = [Calcite], area = A0*(m/m0)^0.6, k1 = 10^(0.198 - 444/TK), k2 = 3.5e-5, A0 = 0.05, m0 = 1e-3

A name given an expression that reads the water — a concentration, an activity, SI() or SR(), I, TK, TC, t, or a name of this kind before it — stands for that expression, in brackets, wherever the law or a later name uses it: a rate written step by step, as a program would work it out. Such a name may use the names before it on its line and the line's constants wherever they are given, and may not be one a rate law reads already. A power's exponent may go with TK or TC, which no species moves.

Calcite => Ca+2 + CO3-2, r = k*A*(1 - SR(Calcite)), k = 1e-7, A = 0.5
=> X, r = kx*{H+}^0.5                     # an activity: gamma times the concentration
=> Y, r = ky*exp(-0.5*I)                  # I, the ionic strength of the cell
A => B, r = k25*exp(-Ea/8.314*(1/TK - 1/298.15))*[A], k25 = 1e-5, Ea = 6e4   # TK, the cell's temperature

A rate law may read more than concentrations. {H+} is the activity of H+, its activity coefficient times [H+], as ACTIVITY works it out (without ACTIVITY, the concentration), and {H2O} the water's when no species is called H2O. I is the ionic strength of the cell, unless a parameter of that name says otherwise, and TK and TC its temperature in kelvin and in °C (see The temperature below). SI(Calcite) is a phase's saturation index, log(IAP/K) in activities, and SR(Calcite) its saturation ratio, IAP/K. The phase is one of <PHASES> below, or a mineral with its relation in <EQUILIBRIUM>. So a mineral can dissolve and precipitate at a rate, as PHREEQC's RATES have it — k·A·(1 − SR), or any law of the saturation — with the amount a mineral species the reaction consumes. Each of these reads every ion of the cell through the ionic strength, and the Jacobian follows it there. A law that should stop when the mineral is gone says so, as (m/m0)^(2/3) does in PHREEQC's examples.

The time unit

A model has one time unit and everything in it is in that unit: the rate constants, the diffusivities, TEND, a velocity. TIME_UNIT says which, and it converts nothing — the solver integrates the numbers it is given. What the setting does is let the page say so: label the time axis, read 500 a in the Times box, print a travel time, head a CSV column. Without it everything is called seconds, which is what a model that says nothing means.

So a geosphere model written in years has half-lives in years, De in m²/a and a velocity in m/a, and writes TIME_UNIT = year; the radiolysis models are in seconds throughout and say nothing. Mixing the two inside one model is the one mistake this cannot catch, and never could: a diffusivity in m²/s beside a half-life in years is simply a different model from the one that was meant.

The two ends of the column

Four kinds of face, under the names the transport literature gives them, and two more. Which you want depends on what is on the other side: a reservoir, a wall, water arriving, or nothing at all — and, for a reservoir, whether its water changes.

dirichlet
first type		 The face is held at the concentration on the species' left= or right=. Diffusion sees that concentration half a cell away, and the flow, if there is any, carries it in. Right for a face against a large well-mixed reservoir. It sets nothing inside the column: every cell, the one touching the face included, starts where <SPECIES> and <INITIAL> put it.
reservoir A dirichlet face whose reservoir reacts. Its water is a cell of its own, outside the column, with the model's whole chemistry — reactions, relations, equilibria held — and no transport: the column takes from it and gives to it across the face without changing it, and only its own reactions do. Diffusion sees it half a cell away and the flow carries it in, as at a dirichlet face. It starts at each species' left= or right=, or else its <SPECIES> value, unless an <INITIAL> line names it, with left or right in place of the cells — Red left 1e-3 fixed — where an expression is read as the cell beside it. It has that cell's parameters, temperature and R, and reacts as that cell would: a source whose strength decays, a nuclide chain whose daughters grow in the water that feeds the column. With nothing reacting it is dirichlet. The charts show the column; what crosses the face is under flux through the left end (or the right).
neumann
second type		 Nothing crosses. A wall.
robin, cauchy
third type		 The flow carries left= in and nothing else crosses: the whole flux through the face is u·c. This is Danckwerts' inlet, and it is the one to use where the boundary is water arriving at a stated concentration rather than a reservoir held at one. It needs a VELOCITY pointing inwards; the page says so if there is none.
free, outflow Zero gradient: no diffusion across the face, and the flow takes what it takes. Danckwerts' outlet, and the companion of robin at the other end. It asks for nothing to be known about what is beyond the face, which at an outlet is the point.
semi-infinite
right only		 The column goes on past its end, and the end is a plane inside it that the flow and the dispersion both cross: what leaves is the whole flux, and the concentration falls to zero only infinitely far downstream. The outlet of the analytical far-field models, FARF31's among them, and of Kompartment's far-field path. EXTRA_CELLS cells as wide as the last stand for the column beyond, the last of them closed by carrying the gradient on through it. It needs the flow to run to the right.

The difference between the first and third types is worth knowing, because it is a common way to get an inlet wrong. A Dirichlet face adds a diffusive flux D(cb − C)/d on top of what the flow brings, so more enters than the water carries, and the column's mass balance does not close: measured on the test column in resources/tests/rtm/, 6.7 % more goes in than u(cin − cout) accounts for. With robin and free the same column balances to one part in 1015, and its profile matches the flux-type closed solution of van Genuchten and Alves, converging on it by a factor of four each time the cells are doubled.

As arithmetic, free and neumann are the same face — the advective outflow applies at every cell whatever the far boundary says, so a Neumann outlet already lets the flow leave. They are separate words because they say different things and are wrong in different ways, and the page can only tell you which is wrong if it knows which you meant.

How many cells a semi-infinite end needs past it is a matter of how far upstream the dispersion reaches. It dies away by D/(D + uΔx) per cell, which for a Peclet number and no D of a species' own is (2N − Pe)/(2N + Pe), and unless EXTRA_CELLS says otherwise they are the fewest that bring that under a tenth: five for twenty cells at Pe 10, as Kompartment counts them, and none where the grid's own dispersion is all there is. They are the column going on, so a line of <PARAMETERS> or <INITIAL> that reaches the last cell reaches them too, and a rock matrix is behind them as well. The charts leave them out.

A Danckwerts outlet, free, closes the column at its end instead, and lets out 4a/(1 + a)² of what the semi-infinite column does at a frequency where a = √(1 + 4twg/Pe). Against FARF31's exact transfer function, the far-field path below is 2.4 % out where most of a release is with a semi-infinite end, and 6.8 % with a free one. More cells bring the first down by four per doubling, to 0.17 % on 160, and make the second worse, 9.9 % on 160: that column closes on Danckwerts' answer, which is not FARF31's.

What crosses an end. On Against time the Cell picker ends with flux through the left end and through the right end: what crosses the plane at 0 or at LENGTH, per m² of the water's cross-section and positive to the right, in concentration times metres per unit of time. At an outlet that is the release — advection alone at a free end, advection and dispersion at a semi-infinite one. It is worked out from the stored run with exactly the terms the equations put through that face, so what the column gains is what crosses its left end less what crosses its right, to round-off.

A rock matrix: dual porosity

Water flows along a fracture; the rock beside it is stagnant, porous, and open to diffusion. A species carried along the fracture leaks sideways into the rock's pore water, sorbs there, and comes back out long after the water that brought it has gone — which is what holds a radionuclide up in a geosphere by orders of magnitude more than the water's own travel time. Say anything about the rock and every cell of the column gets a chain of stagnant cells behind it, twelve unless MATRIX_CELLS says otherwise (0 for none), reaching MATRIX_DEPTH metres into the rock. A far-field path is written the way the SKB reports describe one, and here as Kompartment's far-field block starts one:

<SETTINGS>

CELLS = 20                  # along the path
LENGTH = 100                # m; any length: TRAVEL_TIME sets the flow
TRAVEL_TIME = 100           # the water's travel time t_w, in the time unit
PECLET = 10
LEFT = robin                # water arriving at left= per m3
RIGHT = semi-infinite       # the rock goes on past the release point
MATRIX_DEPTH = 12.5         # m into the rock
MATRIX_POROSITY = 0.0018
MATRIX_DENSITY = 2700       # kg/m3, the default
TRANSPORT_RESISTANCE = 1E5  # F = a_w t_w: 1000 m2 of wall per m3 of water here

<SPECIES>
Cs  0  left=1  De=1E-4  Kd=0.01    # De in m2/a, Kd in m3/kg

Each species has its De= (or Dm=, the same thing), the effective diffusivity in the rock, and a capacity there: Kd=, the partition coefficient, makes it εm + ρmKd with MATRIX_POROSITY and MATRIX_DENSITY, and Rm= gives it outright and may name a parameter, so that it varies from cell to cell. With neither the species has the porosity alone, which is one that does not sorb; with no De it never enters the rock. Kdf= is sorption on the fracture coating, which retards the species in the water by R = 1 + Kd,faw; R= says it outright.

The flow-wetted surface aw, the area of fracture wall a cubic metre of flowing water touches, is given one of three ways: WETTED_SURFACE itself, in m²/m³; APERTURE (2b) for a fracture of two walls, as 2/aperture; or TRANSPORT_RESISTANCE, the F of the SKB reports, as F/tw. Two of them given have to agree. TRAVEL_TIME gives the flow as the water's travel time; without it tw is LENGTH over the pore-water speed. A unit release into the path is a robin left end with left at one over the pore-water speed, or a source => X, k = S with S 0 1/Δx fracture in <PARAMETERS>; the release out is what crosses the right end.

Concentrations are per unit pore water everywhere, and so are the rate laws. A cubic metre of rock holds only εm of water, so a rate r per unit water changes the rock's inventory by εmr, and it is the inventory, RmC, that the rock's equation is written for: Rm dC/dt = εmr + diffusion. That is right for chemistry in the water and wrong for one thing: radioactive decay, which does not ask where an atom sits, sorbed or dissolved. Write such a rate with on = inventory and it removes the same share of everything in the cell, and makes as much of what is on the right: a daughter born of a parent that sorbs hard is born as many atoms as the parent lost, and then takes up its own share between water and rock, as Kompartment and FARF31 have it. Such a reaction has one species on the left, or none.

Cs => , k = 7.3E-10, on = inventory     # decay: of everything, sorbed included

A line in <INITIAL> or <PARAMETERS> names cells of the column. Add matrix after it to mean the rock behind those cells instead, or fracture to mean the flowing water alone. A parameter with neither applies to both — right for a temperature, wrong for a source into the water, which would otherwise also feed every layer of rock behind that cell. In a parameter's expression j is the layer (0 in the fracture) and xm the depth to its centre in metres.

How fine. With matched layers, twelve do as well as twenty: what is left is the cells along the path, and they converge on the exact answer at second order. A release history

A source that follows the clock is a reaction with nothing on the left whose rate reads a table at the time, t: what a near field releases into a far-field path, year by year, as another code worked it out or as a report tabulates it.

<TABLE release>
t           U238      U234          # the first row names the columns; t in years
0           0         0
1000        2.0E-3    1.5E-3
1.0E4       1.1E-3    9.0E-4
1.0E5       0         0

<PARAMETERS>
IN  all  0
IN  0    1/w  fracture              # into the first cell: one per m2 of water's cross-section

=> U238, r = IN*release(t, U238)

IN turns a release per m² of the water's cross-section into one per m³ of the first cell's water; what crosses the right end, on Against time, is then per m² too, and the two compare directly. The time is in the model's own unit, and a table is held at its last row beyond it: end a history with zeros if it ends. The argument may be any arithmetic on t and the line's constants — release(t - 1000) to start it a thousand years late, or release(t/3.156e7) to read a history in years in a model in seconds.

The run is started again at every corner of such a table — every row, moved and scaled with the argument when that is a straight line in t. A stiff solver that has grown its steps over a quiet stretch would otherwise step clean over a short pulse and never see it: a release of ten years in five thousand comes out as nothing at all without the restarts, and whole with them. Kompartment does the same at its switch times. The panel says how many corners there are, and the status line how many the run was started at; a history of a few hundred rows costs a few thousand steps.

Through Kompartment's default far-field path, a history that rises, holds and falls over twenty thousand years comes out as FARF31 convolves it with the path's exact response, to the accuracy of the cells along the path: for Ra-226, which the rock holds back by five orders, the peak is 30 % high on 20 cells, 7 % on 40 and 1.8 % on 80.

What may go in an expression

The same arithmetic in a rate law, a constant and a parameter: + - * /, brackets, and ^ or ** for a power. Numbers may be written 1.4E11 or 1.4e11. The functions are:

exp(a)ea.
log(a), ln(a)Natural logarithm — log means the natural one here, as it does in FACSIMILE.
log10(a)Base ten.
sqrt(a)Square root.
abs(a), sign(a)Magnitude and sign.
step(a)1 where a is above zero, 0 elsewhere.
ramp(a)a where it is above zero, 0 elsewhere — max(a, 0) by another name.
min(a,b), max(a,b), pow(a,b)In <PARAMETERS> only; see below.

Three of those are barred from a rate law, and the reason is the Jacobian rather than taste. min and max have a derivative that jumps where their two arguments cross, so an analytic Jacobian would be wrong on one side of the crossing; pow(a,b) differentiates to something containing log a, which is undefined at a zero concentration — which is exactly where a rate law gets evaluated. A power whose exponent does not depend on a concentration — [A]^2, [A]^(2/3), [A]^n with n a constant or a parameter — has neither problem and is what to write. <PARAMETERS> is worked out once when the model compiles and never differentiated, so all three may be used there.

step and ramp are allowed, with the derivative of the kink taken as zero: they are flat everywhere they are differentiable, and a stiff solver steps over the corner rather than through it. A rate that switches hard is still worth avoiding where a smooth one will do.

Which names are in scope depends on where the expression is. A rate law may use the concentrations it writes in brackets, the constants named on its own line, any parameter, t, the time in the model's own unit, and a table read at that time — see A release history below; a constant on the reaction line wins if a parameter has the same name, and no parameter may be called t. A parameter, and a starting value in <INITIAL>, may use x, the cell centre in metres, w, the cell's width, xl and xr, its two faces, i, the cell index, the tables, and any parameter named above it.

Optional, and only useful with transport: it overrides the concentration in <SPECIES> cell by cell, which is how you start a front, a plume or a layer. The value may be an expression in what a parameter can use, worked out in each cell — so an amount per m² of surface goes in as itself divided by w, and stays the same amount per m² whatever the grid.

fixed at the end of the line holds the species in those cells and nowhere else, as fixed on its species line holds it everywhere: its row is zero there, so nothing — reaction, diffusion, a face — can move it, and it still feeds its neighbours and drives its reactions. That is a reservoir cell, or a surface species that must not appear in the water. With no value, U_site 1-9 fixed holds it where it already is. A species held in some cells only cannot be put on the <EQUILIBRIUM> relations by EQUILIBRATE, and the page says so rather than moving it anyway.

This is how a tabulated dose-rate profile goes in as it is, where skbrtm fits a function to it first — and a fit is only as good as where it was started: skbrtm's beta and gamma fits come out as straight lines, 30 % low at the surface.

A surface

A dissolving surface — sites, a coating, a reacting film — is written as species that live in the first cell, stated per m² of surface and spread through that cell: U_site 0 2.1e-4/w/1000 in <INITIAL>, with no D so that they stay put. The amount per m² is then right on any grid.

What the chemistry does there is another matter. A rate law is written in concentrations, so a reaction between two surface species goes as the square of something divided by the cell's width, and when that width comes from the grid, refining the grid moves the answer: in skbrtm's fuel-dissolution example the dissolved uranium per m² halves each time the cells are halved. SURFACE_LAYER pins the first cell's thickness, making it a property of the model — a reaction zone, as the batch model's 40 µm alpha layer is — and lays the rest of the column out behind it. Refine the grid then and the surface does not change.

This is how a dose rate that dies away from a surface is written, or a porosity that changes down a column, or a rate constant that follows a temperature profile. A rate law may use a parameter by name, exactly as it uses a constant declared on its own line; a constant on the reaction line wins if both have the same name.

A relation that has to hold rather than a rate that has to be integrated: ∏[C]ν = K, with logK its base-ten logarithm, which is the form a database gives. dH, the enthalpy of reaction in kJ/mol, or analytic, an analytic expression, makes the log K go with the temperature, as a PHREEQC database's delta_h and -analytic do: see The temperature below. An empty left-hand side means the reaction has no reactant, as it does for water dissociating. H2O, when it is not one of the species, is the water itself, and a relation may be written with it as a database writes it: H2O <=> H+ + OH-, CO2 + H2O <=> H+ + HCO3-. It is never a concentration. Its activity is 1, which leaves the relation as it would be without it, unless ACTIVITY says otherwise.

With EQUILIBRATE = 1 the starting concentrations are put on these relations before the run: a damped Newton iteration in ln C, run separately in every cell, over the species the relations mention and no others — anything they do not touch is left exactly where it was put, zero included. What it may not change is worked out from the relations themselves — the left null space of their stoichiometry, which gives the conserved totals without the page needing a table of elements. So writing H+ at 10-3 and OH- at 10-3 gives 10-7 of each, and the difference between them, which is a charge, is exactly what it was.

During the run a relation can be kept in two ways. Give it a kf and it becomes a fast reversible pair, with kb = kf/K: two reactions that pull it back to equilibrium whenever something moves it off. A kf a few orders above the fastest rate in the model holds the relation to within the solver's tolerance, and making it enormous only costs steps. But a pair always lags a little where the water changes fast, as it does in the first cell of a column taking a new water.

With EQUILIBRATE = always the relations given no kf are held exactly instead. In every cell each is then an algebraic equation, Σ ν ln C = ln K, and the species they move are no longer integrated one by one. What is integrated in their place are the totals the relations conserve, the same totals the start is speciated with: a reaction or a flux between cells changes a total as it changes the species in it, and the species are worked out from the totals at every step. That is a speciation in every cell, solved together with the reactions and the transport in one implicit system. The relations then hold to rounding at every stored point, and what they conserve is conserved to rounding. A species with an R counts in a total R times, so what is conserved is the inventory. A relation with a kf stays a fast pair beside the held ones, and a fixed species in a held relation must be above zero in every cell.

Such a system has algebraic equations in it, which only the NDF solves: Auto hands it to the NDF, and the other methods decline it. Each held species is solved for as ln C, and an error of rtol in ln C is a relative error of rtol in C, so the tolerance means for these species what it means for the others.

With ACTIVITY the relations of <EQUILIBRIUM> hold between activities, Σ ν log (γC) = log K, and each logK is then the thermodynamic constant a database gives rather than one made for a particular water. Every activity coefficient γ follows the ionic strength of its cell, I = ½ Σ z²C over all the cell's species, as the run goes on:

  • an ion, by Davies: log γ = −A z² (√I/(1 + √I) − 0.3 I);
  • under debye-huckel, an ion given a=, by the WATEQ Debye–Hückel form: log γ = −A z² √I/(1 + B a √I) + b I, with a= in ångström and b= in kg/mol, a database's -gamma;
  • a neutral species: log γ = b I, with b 0.1 unless b= gives it;
  • a species named H2O: γ = 1.

That is PHREEQC's rule, and on the same water the page's activity coefficients are PHREEQC's to rounding: 6×10−16 in log γ from a dilute water to 3 mol/kgw of NaCl, and from 25 to 150 °C. A and B come from each cell's temperature, TEMPERATURE, as PHREEQC works them out, from the dielectric constant and the density of water; from 0 to 200 °C they are PHREEQC's to nine digits. The water's activity is 1 − 0.017 ΣC, PHREEQC's too, and as in PHREEQC it moves A and B a little: PHREEQC takes the density of water at the vapour pressure times the water's activity, Raoult's law, and above 100 °C the water at that pressure, so a salty or hot water's A and B are its own. Where the water can be above 100 °C that part of them is in the Jacobian; below, it is a part in 105 of a coefficient's derivative at most, and is left out. A charge is read from the end of the name, H+ 1 and SO4-2 −2, and z= gives it where the name should not: a surface site is no ion in the water, and z=0 b=0 leaves it out of the ionic strength with γ = 1. Concentrations are read as molalities.

The activities act on the relations, wherever the page uses them: the speciation of the start, the relations held throughout the run, and the fast pairs, whose backward constant follows the ionic strength so that each pair relaxes to its K in activities. A rate law in <REACTIONS> stays in concentrations. A relation then reads every ion in its cell, through the ionic strength, so the Jacobian is fuller within a cell, and a little more work.

For CO2 that is about 6×10−4 in log K for each atmosphere the pressure moves.

PHREEQC's SOLID_SOLUTIONS. A solid solution's end members are minerals (see Minerals), each in the one relation that dissolves it with the pure phase's log K, and each of one solid solution only. In it a member's activity is not 1 but its mole fraction x in the solid solution times λ: the members' amounts are its composition, and their relations hold the water at it. In an ideal solid solution λ is 1, and so it is in one of three members or more. Two members may be given Guggenheim's a0 and a1, dimensionless, as PHREEQC's -Gugg_nondimensional gives them, or g0 and g1 in kJ/mol, its -Gugg_kJ, and then, as PHREEQC writes them,

ln λ1 = x2² (a0 − a1 (3 − 4 x2))
ln λ2 = x1² (a0 + a1 (4 x2 − 1))

x2 the second member's mole fraction. They are given at 25 °C, or at tc=; at any other temperature a0 and a1 go as 1/T, their energies a R T held, as in PHREEQC.

A solid solution is there or not as a whole. It is there while the water holds it at saturation — each member's IAP/K over its λ, at the composition the water gives the solid, adding up to 1, which for two members is Lippmann's total solubility product — and each member's amount is then its share of what the totals leave. With EQUILIBRATE the start is put on it as PHREEQC puts a water on its SOLID_SOLUTIONS: where the water is supersaturated in it, it precipitates, at the composition the water then gives it; where there is some of it and the water is undersaturated, it dissolves until the water is saturated or none is left. With EQUILIBRATE = always that goes on through the run, as for a mineral: where the last of it dissolves the run stops at that instant, and every member's amount is held at zero; where the water becomes supersaturated in it again the run stops there and it precipitates. The status names it: the solid solution CaSrCO3 precipitates. A member counts in the totals, and under ACTIVITY its relation reads every ion of its cell, as a mineral's does.

Where a0 and a1 are large enough, the two members do not mix in every proportion. The page finds the miscibility gap at each cell's temperature as PHREEQC's ss_prep does, step for step: none above the solid solution's critical temperature, and below it the spinodal and the two compositions the gap lies between, where PHREEQC's search stops, at 10−6 in the activities. Where TEMPERATURE goes with time, the page then takes the two compositions on to round-off: PHREEQC's stopping point moves in jumps of up to 10−6 as the temperature moves, and a run held at the gap's edge cannot step across a jump. A composition inside the gap is two solids, one at each of them: the water is held at the activities the gap's edge gives each member, as PHREEQC holds it, and the amounts move between them. Where the composition comes to the gap, from either side, or leaves it, the run stops there and goes on, and the status says so: CaSrCO3 comes to its miscibility gap. Aragonite and strontianite, with Plummer and Busenberg's a0 = 3.43 and a1 = −1.82 as in PHREEQC's tenth example, have one between strontianite fractions of 0.0048 and 0.858 at 25 °C.

A relation's log K goes with temperature as PHREEQC's does, by its k_calc, T in kelvin:

log K = logK − dH (298.15 − T)/(ln 10 · R T · 298.15)
        + A1 + A2 T + A3/T + A4 log10 T + A5/T² + A6 T²

logK is its log K at 25 °C, dH its enthalpy of reaction in kJ/mol, held constant (van 't Hoff's equation), analytic = A1 A2 A3 A4 A5 A6 its analytic expression, up to six terms and the rest zero, and R PHREEQC's 0.00831470 kJ/(mol K). An analytic expression with a term not zero replaces logK and dH, as PHREEQC's databases have it: one may give all three, and the expression is the relation's log K. A relation with logK alone has it at every temperature. So does a phase of <PHASES>.

TEMPERATURE is a number of degrees, or an expression of the parameters and the clock: a parameter, TEMPERATURE = Tc with Tc all 10 + 3*i, for a temperature that differs from cell to cell; a table read at the clock, heat(t), for one that goes with time, the run started again wherever the table turns; or both, Tc + rise(t). Each cell's temperature sets, there and then, every relation's log K, the Debye–Hückel A and B of ACTIVITY (and SIT's), a gas's R T in a fixed volume and Peng and Robinson's terms for a real gas, a double layer's √(8000 R T ε ε₀) and its dielectric constant, a binary solid solution's a0, a1 and miscibility gap, Pitzer's parameters and Aφ, a fast pair's backward constant kf/K, and a saturation index in a rate law. A rate law reads it as TK, in kelvin, and TC, in °C: k25*exp(-Ea/8.314*(1/TK - 1/298.15)) is Arrhenius's law. It is worked out for 0 to 350 °C, as the water's A and B are, and a temperature outside that stops the run and says where.

A held relation then holds at the temperature of its cell at each instant: a water heated along a table is, at every stored time, the speciation of its totals at that time's temperature, and a mineral precipitates or dissolves as its solubility moves. Where a heated water goes over from gypsum to anhydrite, the run stops at the temperature where PHREEQC's saturation index of anhydrite crosses zero — 54.38 °C for a dilute water at calcite saturation — and goes on with anhydrite (see Minerals above).

The temperature is what TEMPERATURE says it is, cell by cell and time by time; it is not carried by the water or conducted through the rock. PHREEQC's TRANSPORT moves a solution's temperature with it unless told to hold each cell's, -thermal_diffusion 1e30 0, which is the page's column. Not carried with the temperature: diffusion coefficients, which stay as given; and SIT's interaction coefficients, which stay as written (sit.dat gives none that go with temperature). The pressure term in log K, which comes in above one atmosphere from the molar volumes of <VOLUMES>, is worked out at each cell's temperature at each instant, as the rest is (see Pressure).

Opening skbrtm and BRUM databases

Open… takes skbrtm's database files as well as a model text: select any of reaction.in, solutions.in, diffusion.in, the sourceterm and doserate files, and the Python script that runs them — or drop them on the page, or drop an example's whole folder, script and databases together; what is in it besides those is passed over. The script says which files are read, in what order, and for how long; without it every database selected is read and TEND is one second, to be set by hand. The files are read as skbrtm reads them — a SOLUTION sets every species in its cells, a species the DIFFUSION block does not name diffuses at 10-9 m²/s, names are the same whatever their case — and become one model text, with rate constants per second as skbrtm integrates them.

Where skbrtm would read the text differently from what it says, the model follows the text and the difference is written at the top of it. Every rate law is read both ways, and one whose arguments skbrtm pastes in so that the value changes is named, with the factor. A Dirichlet end holds the face here, where skbrtm holds the first cell; where anything reacts it is a reservoir, since the cell skbrtm holds goes on reacting, and what skbrtm puts in that cell starts the reservoir; a REGRESSION becomes a <TABLE> of its own data, where skbrtm fits a function to it; a constant species becomes fixed, in the cells its SOLUTION names; and a lone cell_id means that cell, where skbrtm puts it in cell 0.

What it actually solves

For every species s in every cell i:

dC/dt = (what the reactions make and consume in cell i)
      + (diffusion across the two faces of cell i)
      + (advection in from upwind)

Diffusion is a finite-volume difference: the flux between two cells over the distance between their centres, divided by the width of the cell it lands in. On an even grid that is the textbook D/Δx²; on an uneven one it stays conservative, which the textbook form does not. Measured on a closed box, mass is held to about one part in 1015, and against the analytic erfc profile the error falls by four each time the cells are doubled — second order, as it should be.

Advection is upwind: a face carries the concentration of the cell upwind of it. That is stable at any step and never makes a new peak or trough, but it spreads a front by v·Δx/2 whatever the model says, as if it had a Peclet number of twice the cells. ADVECTION_SCHEME = limited adds to each face the slope of the cell upwind of it, times the distance to the face, the slope an average of the gradients on either side, 2a²b²(a+b)/(a²+b²)², and none where they differ in sign. That is second order where the profile is smooth, and upwind at a peak or a trough, so nothing goes below zero; as a limiter it lies inside the TVD region, and unlike van Albada's it starts from zero with zero slope, so the Jacobian is smooth and one a difference of the state can check. The first and last cells stay upwind, so what crosses the ends is as upwinding has it, and with PECLET nothing is taken back for the scheme. On a front at a Peclet number of 400, the largest error against the closed solution is 8 % on 40 cells and 0.7 % on 160, where upwinding's is 19 % and 2.9 %; it costs more steps, the Jacobian moving with the profile.

Diffusion that keeps the charge

Each species diffuses at its own D. That is right for a neutral molecule and wrong for ions whose coefficients differ: H+ diffuses more than four times as fast as Cl−, so acid spreading into water runs ahead of its chloride and the water between them is charged, which real water does not allow. DIFFUSION = nernst-planck couples the ions so that no current flows: across each face between two cells an ion moves at

J_k = −D_k ∇C_k + D_k z_k C_k (Σ z_j D_j ∇C_j) / (Σ z_j² D_j C_j)

the second term the pull of the diffusion potential, which makes the charges' fluxes cancel. A fast ion drags its slow partner along and is held back by it: HCl spreading into water does so at 2·DHDCl/(DH + DCl), 3.3×10−9 m²/s, and the page matches the closed solution for that to 2×10−5 of the step, the water neutral to 10−13, where without it the chloride lags the acid by a sixth of the step. Where every coefficient is the same the term is nothing in a neutral water. The charge comes from the name, z= where it should not; a PECLET dispersion moves every species alike and is left as it is. It acts between the cells of the column and at a Dirichlet or reservoir end, between the water held there and the cell beside it, half a cell away; the rock matrix keeps each species on its own.

The gradients are of concentrations unless DIFFUSION_GRADIENT = activity, which needs ACTIVITY: then each is the difference in concentration times 1 + Δln γ/Δln C between the two cells, the factor PHREEQC's multicomponent diffusion takes, which comes to the difference in activity where the profile is smooth. PHREEQC uses the factor only between 0.2 and 2.25 — outside them where a species is nearly level and its activity coefficient is not — and drops it to 1 beyond. The flux then jumps, upwards as the difference falls below 0.2; PHREEQC's explicit steps step over that and an implicit solver cannot (acid against salt, pure water held at both ends: two million steps take that rule to 54 hours). Here the factor is held at 0.2 or 2.25 beyond them: a level species stays level, as in PHREEQC, but gets there at 0.2 or 2.25 of its D where PHREEQC takes 1. A tracer level across a column with sea-strength salt held at one end and fresh water at the other comes out up to 8 % above PHREEQC's in the middle, and the pH 0.008 apart. A species one side has none of has no factor in PHREEQC; since a water from PHREEQC carries a trace of what it lacks, the factor fades out here as one side falls from a millionth of the other to 10−10 of it.

Reading the charts

The time chart draws what is ticked in the list above it, at one cell, or what crosses one end of the column (see The two ends of the column). Against distance draws one species or quantity across all the cells, at several times. Which times is up to you: the Times field takes a list — 0, 1 h, 30 d, 500 y, 2 PV for two pore volumes, or bare numbers in the model's own unit — and leaving it empty spreads eight over the run. Each log concentration axis, on Gradient its colour scale, can start where you say: from after its box takes a concentration such as 1e-10, below which a curve drops out of view, and left empty reaches the least drawn. A run with the electron can span 150 decades, methane at 10−144 where the water is oxic, and the floor keeps the curves that matter readable; what is saved keeps every value. First in its Show are those ticked against time, together at one time — the first of the Times, or the end of the run — each in its colour on the time chart, pH, pe and the like on an axis of their own on the right: a plume's redox zones side by side.

Under Against distance's bar a slider steps through the stored points, one time at a time: the chart draws what Show has at that time — one curve, or those ticked against time — on axes that span the whole run, so that a front moves across a still frame. Play moves it on from where it is, or from the start, at the Pace chosen: the whole run in 5, 10 or 30 seconds, evenly in time, or a stored point a frame, which is slower where the solver stored more. What <DATA> measured is drawn faint, and as itself at the stored time nearest its own. Back to Times, or typing Times, draws the Times again.

Time in, on the bars of Against time, Against distance and Gradient, is one choice for the three: time in the model's own unit, or — where the water flows — in pore volumes, the volumes of water that have flowed into the column, each as much as its pores hold. A pore volume is the time the water takes to cross the column: TRAVEL_TIME, or LENGTH over the pore water's speed, |VELOCITY|/POROSITY. The flow is one number for the whole run, so the curves are the same and only the axis counts differently. Column experiments are read this way: a tracer reaches the outlet after about one pore volume, and a species held back by sorption with a retardation factor R after about R. A run kept beside is drawn in its own pore volumes, so that two runs at different speeds line up where the same water has flowed in: with the dispersion set by PECLET, a dispersivity, a tracer at twice the speed draws the first run's curves. The from boxes and the bare numbers of Times are read in it, and turned into the other when it changes; the CSV of Against time gains a column of pore volumes before the time.

Cells, after Cell on Against time, draws what is ticked at several cells at once — 4, 9, 14, end, end the last — the breakthrough curves down a column. Each keeps its colour, in steps of lightness down the column, faintest at the cell nearest the inlet, so that the further along the flow, the more a curve stands out; six cells at most, as more steps of one colour cannot be told apart. The legend and the CSV give each cell's distance from the left-hand face. Left empty, the one cell Cell chooses is drawn, or what crosses an end.

Breakthrough numbers, in a column, puts two tables under the time chart. Of each curve drawn: where it starts, the farthest it gets from there and when, and when it is first half-way from the one to the other, between stored points — read off every stored point, whatever the axes leave out. Where the water flows, that time is given in pore volumes too, and with it the retardation it implies: those pore volumes over the share of the column the water has crossed to get there, so that what moves with the water reads 1, and a species sorbed linearly with a retardation factor R reads R times what a tracer reads. The half-way is a front's median rather than its mean, and a front that spreads much moves it a little — in a short or dispersive column even a tracer reads a little off 1 — so a species is best set against a tracer in the same run. Of each species and each element's total ticked: what has come out of the column, from its budget as the Composition tab keeps it — what was there at the start, what came in at the left, what went out at the right, what is there at the end and what reactions made or took — and the share gone out of what was there and came in. A run kept beside has rows of its own, and Excel has both tables on sheets of their own.

Units, on each chart's bar and on the water's listing, is one choice for all of them: the unit a concentration is shown in. Under an ACTIVITY a concentration is mol/kgw, and is shown in mmol, µmol or nmol per kgw, or in mg or µg per kgw by its formula mass: a species by its name, CaHCO3+ 101.094 g/mol by the standard atomic weights, and a nuclide by its mass number, U238 238 g/mol, which is its atomic mass to about 0.1 % (tritium's to 0.6 %); a mineral by what its relation makes of it, the water in it counted — Ferrihydrite + 3 H+ <=> Fe+3 + 3 H2O weighs it as Fe(OH)3, 106.866 g/mol; an element's total, or a valence state's, by the element's atomic weight, tot(Ca) at 1 mmol/kgw 40.078 mg/kgw; the sulfate's as SO4. Without an ACTIVITY a concentration is in whatever unit the text means, and is shown times 103, 106 or 109 — never weighed. pH, pe, a saturation index and the like are as they are. What has no such unit — a gas's partial pressure, the alkalinity in mg, which would need saying what it is counted as, a species whose name is no formula (CaX2, Hfo_wOH) in mg — is left off a chart of several and drawn as the model has it on a chart of one, and the note says which. What <DATA> measured and a run kept beside are shown in the same unit, a log axis's from is read in it, and the axis titles and what is saved say it; the listing gains a column in it after PHREEQC's own.

The list holds the species first, then what the run comes to besides them, in groups: the water — pH as −log a(H+), pe, and Eh in volts, the pe times ln 10 RT/F at the water's temperature with the R and F PHREEQC prints its redox couples' Eh with, 0.059156 V a pe at 25 °C; the ionic strength, a(H2O), the charge balance as PHREEQC's percent error, and the temperature where it varies; with <CONDUCTANCE> the specific conductance SC in µS/cm, the density rho in kg/L and the viscosity viscos in mPa s; and where there are Na and Ca or Mg, the sodium adsorption ratio SAR, the dissolved Na over the square root of half the dissolved Ca and Mg, all in meq/kgw; the totals in the water of each element two species or more hold, from their formulas — CaHCO3+ is one Ca and one C, UO2_2(OH)2+2 two U — and, in a model with the electron, of an element whose dissolved species are in two valence states or more, its total in each, as PHREEQC's TOT("Fe(2)"): tot(Fe(3)), tot(Fe(2)), each species' state told as the redox ladder tells it (see Diagrams of the model's own chemistry); saturation indices of each phase of <PHASES> and each mineral; a gas's partial pressure; each species' share of its exchanger, as an equivalent fraction, or of its sites on a surface; the text's <OUTPUT>; what each reaction does, each <REACTIONS> line's rate and its extent (below), shut until it is opened where there are more than six lines; and under an ACTIVITY the log activities, a group shut until it is opened. They are worked out from the stored concentrations with the model's own activity model, in the background the first time a chart asks for them, and kept for the run. A handed-off water's pH, ionic strength, a(H2O), conductance, density, viscosity, totals, saturation indices and the shares of an exchanger and a surface are PHREEQC's to a part in 108, a gas's partial pressure to 3 in 108. An amount goes on the left-hand axis with the species, log or not; pH, pe, Eh, a saturation index, a log activity or a share goes on the right, as it is, the axis naming each with its unit where it has one — Eh (V), specific conductance (µS/cm), a reaction's rate. What <DATA> measured of what is drawn appears as open circles in its colour: a table in time at the cell and layer it names, one in distance with the time it was taken at.

What each reaction does is a group of two for each line of <REACTIONS>. Its rate is the forward rate less the backward one, worked out at each stored point by the very code the solver runs, in the cell's own terms: times what the cell holds of the species for a rate on the inventory, and times the porosity in the rock, so that times the cell's water it is what the reaction moves there in a unit of time — what the budget on the Composition tab counts as made by reactions. It goes on the right, as pH does. Its extent is what it has done since the start, the rate summed over the stored points by the trapezoidal rule, as close as they are to each other. On Gradient a rate shows where a reaction runs and when — the front where organic carbon meets an oxidant, a mineral dissolving behind an acid front — and an extent along the column at the end, how much it did where. A held equilibrium has no rate of its own and is not among them. Diagrams, Sweeps and Uncertainty, which read a quantity point by point, offer the rates and not the extents.

Gradient puts the whole run in one picture: time across, distance up, concentration as colour. Where Against distance shows a handful of moments, this shows every stored one, so a front's arrival and how far it gets are a shape rather than a set of curves to compare. Distance runs up the axis from zero at the origin, so the left-hand face — the surface the chemistry happens at — is along the bottom. As draws it as a map, as contours with their values written on them — the pH of an acid front through calcite, a saturation index going from below zero to zero — or as a surface in three dimensions, the value up, which the mouse turns.

In a model with the electron, Show on Gradient offers first the redox zones: each cell at each stored time coloured by the redox couple that holds its pe — of the couples between two valence states of an element, as the redox ladder has them, the one that takes or gives the most electrons for a change in pe there, its poising capacity n·ToxTred/(Tox + Tred), n the electrons between the two states and T what the cell holds in each, in its water, its minerals and on an exchanger or a surface. The water's own O2 holds it with four electrons to each O2, and H2 with two to each H2, the water the other state of each. The zones are in the order the couples turn over, each read off the run where its two states come nearest even — oxic first, then down the ladder — coloured from the Colours chosen at equal steps of lightness, the oxic water nearest the page's own colour and the more reduced a zone the more it stands out. Hovering gives the couple and the pe; the note gives each zone's share of the waters and the couples that hold the pe nowhere; CSV and Excel give the zones by name. It is a map only: contours, a surface, log concentration and smooth do not apply to it.

The bands across it are the cells: with twenty of them the picture has twenty rows and says so. Smooth interpolates between them, which looks better and claims a resolution the run does not have. log concentration plots log10, leaving anything at or below zero blank rather than dropping it to the bottom of the scale, and the note says how many that was. The from time matters more here than elsewhere: a stiff run's first steps are femtoseconds, and without it fifteen decades of an empty axis push the interesting part into the last inch.

A time you ask for is not usually a time the solver stopped at, and nothing is invented to fill the gap: the nearest profile that was actually computed is drawn, the legend names the time it belongs to rather than the time you asked for, and the note under the toolbar says which times moved. Late in a stiff run the steps are wide — tens of thousands of seconds on the built-in model — so ask for 10 hours there and you may well get 10.8.

What the water is made of

Composition reads the stored concentrations through what each species is made of. A name says it — CaHCO3+ is a Ca, a C, an H and three O, CaX2 a Ca on two of the exchanger's sites — and a mineral's is what its relation makes of the rest: Calcite <=> Ca+2 + CO3-2 is one Ca and one C. A nuclide's name, an element and its mass number, is one atom of the element: U238 and U234 are each one U, Ra226+2 one Ra — a number near the element's atomic weight is read as its mass number, where a formula's count is far from it (O2, S8, C60), and tritium's H3 is one H. Where an element is stacks each species with any of it as its share of all of it, at one cell along the run or along the column at one time: in the water, or in the whole cell, the exchanger, a surface, the minerals and a gas with it. Hydrogen and oxygen are the water's own and are left out, and a species that never comes to 1 % is drawn with the others like it, as one band.

Along the column, a slider under the bar steps through the stored points, as on Against distance: where an element is, and each diagram — Piper, Durov, Schoeller, Stiff, USSL, Wilcox, principal components — drawn for the column at that one time, and play moves it on through the run at the Pace chosen, so that the waters move across the diagram as the run goes. The axes hold still: the triangles and the diamond are still of themselves, and the rest are drawn to what every stored point of every cell comes to — the Schoeller's and the Stiff's scales, the Durov's pH and dissolved solids, the USSL's conductance and SAR, the Wilcox's cations, an element's amounts and which of its species are bands of their own. Principal components are those of the whole run's waters, each time's put on them, so that the arrows stay where they are. A run kept beside moves with it, at its own stored time nearest. Back to At, or typing At, draws At's time again.

The water, as PHREEQC lists it sets one water out as PHREEQC prints a solution: one cell at one stored point, the end of the run unless At says another, from the model's own speciation. Its composition, each element dissolved; its description — pH, pe, Eh, temperature, ionic strength, the water's activity, the carbonate's alkalinity, the conductance, density and viscosity where the model has <CONDUCTANCE>, and the charge balance as PHREEQC's percent error; the distribution of species, H+ and OH- first, then each element, in each of its valence states where the model tells two or more, each species with its molality, activity, their logs and the log of its activity coefficient, a species of two elements under both, as PHREEQC has it; the saturation index of each phase and mineral, with its log IAP and the log K of its dissolution at the water's temperature; the minerals' amounts; what is on an exchanger or a surface, with its share; and a gas's partial pressure. Without an ACTIVITY each activity coefficient is 1. Text saves it in PHREEQC's columns, to set beside PHREEQC's own output; CSV the distribution of species, and Excel every block on a sheet of its own. Beside PHREEQC gives the same water to PHREEQC, through the database chosen on the PHREEQC tab and as that tab gives it a run's waters — its element totals, the pH walked onto its [H+], its pe — and sets PHREEQC's numbers beside the model's: each species' molality and log gamma, the difference in log molality, each phase's saturation index, and the water's pH, ionic strength, activity and percent error. The species PHREEQC finds that the model has not are listed apart, and the note says how far apart the two are at most and what PHREEQC was not given. A water handed off from PHREEQC agrees to rounding; a constant of the model's own shows as the saturation index it moves.

A budget over the run keeps account of an element, or of one species: at each stored time how much of it the column holds — in the water, on an exchanger or a surface, in each mineral, and in the rock matrix — stacked, with what has gone out through the right-hand end on top, and dashed beside them what was there at the start with what has come in through the left. Each species counts by its capacity in its cell (R= in the column, Rm= in the rock) and by the cell's water: the width of a cell, and a layer of rock as the fracture's wall area per its water times the layer's thickness; per m² of the water's cross-section, as the flux through an end is. The flux through each end is summed over the stored points by the trapezoidal rule. What the reactions made of it is the gap between the two, drawn dotted: none for an element chemistry only moves about, where the gap is the solver's tolerance and the summing's, which the note gives; a nuclide's decay, or a source among the reactions, where they make or take it. Every element, first in Of, sets the balance of each element side by side: what was there at the start, came in, went out, is there at the end and what the reactions made, and how far it is off its balance, the most the reactions made or took of it at any stored time as a share of the most the column held or was given. Below 10−4 it closes; above, a reaction makes or takes it — the check that a run kept what it should, at a glance.

By each reaction, where the model has <REACTIONS>, it splits what the reactions made line by line. Each line's rate of making it and of taking it is worked out at each stored point as the solver's derivative works it out: the line's rates times its coefficients, each species weighed as the budget weighs it. A line and one written apart that undoes it — the same species, each coefficient the other way — are one reaction, what the two do net: a fast equilibrium written as two lines would otherwise make and take far more than ever happens and hide what does. They are drawn stacked over the whole column or at one cell (Over): what the reactions make above nought, what they take below, the eight that move most of it named and the rest together, and dotted, made less taken by every reaction. Under the chart, over the run, what each made and took and its share, with its lines in the text. Over the whole column the table goes on with what the relations moved, relation by relation, held or as fast pairs: for every species, what the budget counts the reactions as having made less what the lines did is what the relations moved of it, and each relation's extent is read off the balance of a species of its own — the one fewest relations share, mostly the species it defines — its part here that extent times what it makes of this. What is left is the summing's error over the stored points, each species' own, which the note gives as a share of what was made and taken; where two relations' moves could stand in for each other, the table gives what the relations moved together instead. A relation moves an element between its species and makes none of it, and so does a line that moves it from one species to another: the organic carbon's oxidation turns one species of carbon into another and makes no carbon.

Where the minerals' fronts are follows each mineral's fronts along a column, from the inlet, through the run. Of a mineral the column holds at the start, the length of column it has been cleared of: each cell counts its width times the share of its first amount that is gone, which for a sharp front from the inlet is where the front is, its cell's share placing it inside the cell, and for a spread one the middle of the spread. Of a mineral that forms where there was none, the zone it has formed in, its edges the first and the last cell holding any. A table gives each front at the end and its speed over the second half of the run, against the water's own speed, the column's length over the time it takes the water to cross it, and when each mineral was gone or first formed. A mineral dissolving into water that carries none of it moves its front at v S/(S + M), v the water's speed, S what the water holds at saturation and M the mineral in each kilogram of water: the front waits while the water carries away what it dissolves.

The water's major ions are drawn as the hydrochemists draw them, in equivalents: Ca and Mg twice their dissolved totals, Na and K together, Cl, the sulfate twice the sulfur in a species with four O to each S — SO4-2, CaSO4, not HS- — and the carbonate's alkalinity, HCO3- one and CO3-2 two, their complexes as many. Under an ACTIVITY the concentrations are mol/kgw and these are meq/kgw; otherwise they are the concentrations times the charge. The two that are not totals of an element are on the time chart too, as alk(CO3) and, where some of the sulfur is something else, tot(SO4), and a table in <DATA> can name them.

A Piper diagram puts the cations' shares in the triangle on the left, the anions' on the right, and the two together in the diamond above, with a grid every 20 %: a water near the diamond's left corner is a Ca–HCO3 water, near its right corner a Na–Cl one. Along the run it is a path through every stored point, coloured by time; along the column, one point per cell, coloured by distance. A Durov diagram (Durov 1948) puts the same shares in a triangle on the left of a square — Na + K at its foot, Ca at its head, Mg at the apex — and in one on top of it — Cl to the left, HCO3 + CO3 to the right, SO4 at the apex — and the water in the square where the two meet, each triangle's point carried straight into it along its third ion's axis: HCO3 + SO4/2 across, Ca + Mg/2 up, so that a Na–Cl water is at the square's foot on the left and a Ca–HCO3 one at its head on the right. Extended, as the programs that draw it extend it, it has the water's pH in a panel under the square, at its place across, and its total dissolved solids in a panel to the right, at its place up: the mass of every species dissolved but the water, from its formula and IUPAC's standard atomic weights, in mg/kgw — which needs the concentrations in mol/kgw, as an ACTIVITY makes them; a species whose name is no formula is left out of it, and the note says which. A Schoeller diagram draws each water as a line through its six ions on a log axis, so that a water diluted keeps its shape and moves down; Stiff diagrams draw each as a polygon, cations to the left of zero, anions to the right, all on one scale. Both take the waters from Times — six spread over the run unless given — or along the column six cells from end to end. Measured waters are read from <DATA> by the names the model gives them, tot(Ca) or Ca+2, alk(CO3) or HCO3-, and drawn beside the model's in black or orange. The note under the toolbar names each water's type, its hydrochemical facies: the cation and the anion that are half or more of theirs, or the two largest where none is.

A USSL diagram classes a water for irrigation as the U.S. Salinity Laboratory did (Richards 1954, USDA Handbook 60; Wilcox 1955, USDA Circular 969): its specific conductance across, on a log scale, its salinity hazard low, medium, high or very high, C1 to C4, split at 250, 750 and 2,250 µS/cm; and its sodium adsorption ratio up, its sodium hazard S1 to S4, split by lines that fall as the conductance rises, a water's sodium doing more harm the saltier it is. It needs SC, which a model has with <CONDUCTANCE> — a water from PHREEQC whose database gives -dw has it — and SAR. Along the run it is a path through the stored points, coloured by time; along the column, a point a cell. Measured waters come from <DATA>: a column SC, and SAR or the Na, Ca and Mg it is worked out from. The table and the note give each water's class, C2-S1 and the like. The classes are for the conductance at 25 °C, and the model's is at the water's own temperature, about 2 % lower for each degree below.

A Wilcox diagram classes a water for irrigation as L. V. Wilcox did (1948, The quality of water for irrigation use, USDA Technical Bulletin 962, figure 1): its total concentration across, the cations in milliequivalents — Ca and Mg twice their dissolved totals, Na and K once — which Wilcox took as about a hundredth of the conductance in µS/cm, the scale along the top; and its percent sodium up, Na over the four cations. Lines at 7.5, 20 and 30 meq/kgw and three curves that fall from 100 % split it into excellent to good, good to permissible, permissible to doubtful, doubtful to unsuitable and unsuitable. Wilcox gave the lines as numbers and drew the curves, each through open circles; the page's curves are traced from the bulletin's scanned figure — the circles' centres found on the scan, the axes set by its frame, its class lines and its ticks, and a monotone cubic between the circles, which lies on the drawn lines to 0.4 % in percent sodium (0.6 % at worst). The Colorado River at Yuma, Wilcox's own example at 12.06 e.p.m. and 39 % sodium, is good to permissible, as he says. It needs the concentrations in mol/kgw, which a model with an ACTIVITY has, and sodium. Along the run it is a path through the stored points, coloured by time; along the column, a point a cell. Measured waters come from <DATA> by the names the model gives the cations, tot(Na) or Na+, one without a column counting as none, and the table and the note give each water's class.

Principal components do what Gibbs Studio's principal component analysis does with a set of waters. Each water — every stored point along the run at a cell, or each cell of the column at a time — is a row of the variables named under Of, as the time chart names them (pH, tot(Ca), Na+, SI(Calcite)), or else of its pH and each element dissolved, an element's total where it has more than one species. With logs of amounts an amount is taken as its logarithm wherever every water has some of it. Each variable is then standardised, its mean taken off and the rest divided by its standard deviation, so that a pH and a total weigh alike, and the components are the eigenvectors of the variables' correlation matrix, the first the direction the waters differ most along. A variable that is the same in every water is left out, and the note says so. The chart puts each water at its scores on the first two components, coloured by time or by distance, and each variable as an arrow to its correlation with the two, drawn to the waters' scale: an arrow along a component is a variable that goes with it. The axes say how much of the variance each component carries. Waters <DATA> measured of every variable are put on the same components, with the model's means and deviations. Waters that mix two end members and nothing else lie on a line: taken as they are, the first component carries all of their variance. The CSV has each water's scores, and Excel adds the loadings and each component's eigenvalue and share.

Diagrams of the model's own chemistry

Diagrams does with the model's relations what PhreePlot does with PHREEQC's. It takes the water of one cell, as the model starts it or as the run leaves it, holds it at each point of a range — a pH, a pe or an Eh, the activity of a dissolved species, a cation's activity over the hydrogen ion's, or an element's total in the cell — and speciates it there with the relations of <EQUILIBRIUM>, its minerals dissolving or precipitating to stay at saturation while there is any of them. A pH or an activity is held through the concentration that gives it, the activity coefficient taken again from the speciation until it stops moving; a pe is the electron's own activity, an Eh the pe it is in volts at the water's temperature; a total moves the one conserved total that carries most of the element and leaves the rest as they were. Nothing else is held: what is not varied keeps its total, as PHREEQC's SOLUTION keeps what it is given, and there is no charge balance. A total varied without the pH holds the pH where the water has it, as a SOLUTION holds the pH it is given; the note says at what. So does a ratio, log a(K+)/a(H+) or log a(Ca+2)/a(H+)², the cation held at the activity that gives it over the H+ of the point: the other axis' pH where that is one. The second thing varied can instead be the pH held at a number of one's own: the water is held at that pH at every point of the first. Each point starts from the one beside it, and from the water itself where that does not settle; a point that settles neither way is tried again from the point after it, the range walked back the other way, and one that still does not is left blank, the status line saying how many.

Varying one thing draws the species of an element as their shares of it — the classic distribution of carbonate against pH — or as log concentrations, the saturation indices of every phase and mineral, or any species or quantity. Varying two draws a map: at each point the species that holds most of an element, which over pH and pe or Eh is a Pourbaix diagram, the water's own limits drawn across it as dashed lines, O2 above and H2 below at one atmosphere and 25 °C — in volts Eh = 1.229 − 0.0592 pH and −0.0592 pH; the phase furthest over saturation, or at it, which over activities is a mineral stability diagram; the phases of an element, the most stable per mole of it — each phase's saturation index over its count of the element, aluminium in a silicate, the largest the phase the rest would turn into with the element kept in the solids, whatever of it is dissolved — which over log a(H4SiO4) and log a(K+)/a(H+) is Garrels and Christ's diagram of K-feldspar, muscovite, kaolinite and gibbsite, a phase without the element that comes to saturation on it, quartz say, dotted across it where it does; or any species or quantity as contours. Against PHREEQC's SOLUTION at the same pH, pe or total, a handed-off water's species agree to 8 in 109. The numbers go to CSV and Excel, a row per point, and the picture to SVG and PNG, or as a live page to HTML.

One thing varied and the water at saturation with a phase draws a solubility diagram, as PhreePlot's species calculations do. At each point the phase's first element, H and O aside — gibbsite's aluminium, calcite's calcium, CO2(g)'s carbon — is set to the total at which the phase's saturation index is 0, or the number written after its name, CO2(g) -3.5, as PHREEQC's SOLUTION sets an element by a phase: Al 1 Gibbsite 0. Several phases, separated by commas, are each a water of their own; with none named, every phase of the model is. What dissolves draws each phase's element dissolved at saturation, its log against what is varied: gibbsite's aluminium falls with the pH as Al3+, is least near pH 6.5 and rises again as Al(OH)4−. With one phase each species of the element is drawn beside it, what it holds of the element, so that they add up to the total. The other views are of the water at saturation with the phase chosen. The total is found by Newton's method in its logarithm, a few speciations a point; a point whose total would lie outside 10−30 to 100 mol/kgw is left blank. Against PHREEQC's SOLUTION of the same water at the same pH, a handed-off water's aluminium at saturation with gibbsite and with amorphous Al(OH)3 agrees to 1 in 108.

Along pe or Eh with the pH held, the redox couples, as a ladder places each of the model's couples where it turns over, as the textbooks' redox ladders do: the pe, or the Eh, at which the water holds as much of the element in the couple's oxidised valence state as in its reduced one. A couple is a relation of <EQUILIBRIUM> with the electron in it between two valence states of an element — SO4-2 + 9 H+ + 8 e- <=> HS- + 4 H2O, sulfur(6) and sulfur(−2), as a database writes its half-reactions — and a state's amount is the element in every species in that state, in the water or, in the whole cell, on an exchanger or a surface and in a mineral as well. A species' valence state is read from its name where that is a formula with one element left over once H is +1 and O −2, the alkalis +1, the alkaline earths +2 and fluorine −1 — FeOH+2 iron(3), NH4+ nitrogen(−3), a mean where it is one, S2O3−2 sulfur(2) — and otherwise from a relation it is in: a complex has its ions' states, FeSO4 iron(2) and sulfur(6), a mineral its ions' too, and one made with the electron what the electrons leave it, pyrolusite manganese(4). A relation with O2 or H2 in it tells no state: its electrons go through the water's own oxygen or hydrogen. Each rung is found between the diagram's points to 10-10 in the axis' units, as a map's boundaries are, and is this water's: its totals, its complexes and its activity coefficients as they are, so that a couple whose two states are not one to one — two NO3− to one N2 — turns over where the element's total puts it. Where water gives way, O2 and H2 at one atmosphere and 25 °C, is dashed across the ladder, and the water itself dotted at its own pe, its own pH beside it. The note says which couples turn over outside the range, which elements the water has none of, and a state no species of it is in. The couples go to CSV and Excel, each with its pe and its Eh at the water's temperature. Against PHREEQC's SOLUTION at each rung, a handed-off water's two valence states hold the same amount of the element to 1 in 109.

Over pH and pe or Eh, the redox couples, as lines traces each rung across the map, the diagram of a water's couples the textbooks draw: each couple's line where the water holds as much of its element in either state, found between the map's points as its boundaries are, a colour to each element and a dash to each of its couples, between where water gives way, dashed, with the water itself marked at its own pH and pe. A couple that does not turn over on the map is said in the note, which state holds its element all over. The lines' points go to CSV and Excel. Against PHREEQC's SOLUTION at the points of each line, a handed-off water's two states hold the same amount of the element to 1 in 109.

A map's fields are the shapes their boundaries bound, as PhreePlot draws them, not the grid's points coloured. Wherever two neighbouring points of the grid are in different fields, the water is held at points between them and speciated again until the boundary is found to 10-10 in the axis' units: where the two species hold the same amount of the element, or where the two phases' saturation indices are the same. A phase of <PHASES> is over saturation where its index is zero or more, and a mineral of the model's own is at saturation where there is any of it. Where three fields meet in one square of the grid, the point they share is solved for. Between the points found, a boundary is drawn straight, so a finer grid follows a curved one more closely, and a field narrower than the grid's spacing can be missed. The boundaries are found in the background once the map is drawn, which shows its points meanwhile; the status line says when they are in, and pointing at the map still reads the nearest point. Excel adds a sheet of them: each boundary's points and the two fields it parts, and each point where three meet. Against PHREEQC's SOLUTION at the points of a handed-off iron water's boundaries, the species either side hold the same amount of iron to 1 in 109.

Sweeps over runs

Sweeps runs the model again for each value of one thing, or each pair of values of two, as Gibbs Studio's parametric runs do: a setting with a number — the velocity, the temperature, how long the run is — a parameter of <PARAMETERS>, a species' concentration at the start on its line, what flows in at the left, its left=, a relation's or a phase's logK, or a number a reaction's line names. Each run is the text with that number put in, everything else as it was, run with the panel's solver settings. The values are a list, or a range — 1e-6 to 1e-4 in 5 log spaces five evenly in the logarithm — and a choice starts from half its value, the value and twice it. A run that fails says why, and the rest go on. A log K starts from a unit either side of its own.

Of each run the page keeps every species and quantity at one cell, at up to 400 of its stored points, and along the column at its end. It draws a species or quantity against what was varied — its value at the end, its largest or its smallest, a point per run — or, with two varied, a curve for each value of the second, contours or a surface over the two; or every run against time at the cell, or along the column at its end, the curves going from dark to light as the value grows.

Each constant in turn, the first choice of Vary where the model has any, says which of its constants a result depends on. Every number a reaction's line names and every parameter given one value throughout is raised by 1 % of itself and lowered by as much, one at a time, and the model is run as written besides: twice as many runs as constants, and one. Of each run the page keeps every species and quantity at the cell, at the end and at its largest and smallest. For the species or quantity chosen it ranks the constants by how much they move it: the change in its logarithm over the change in theirs, between the run 1 % up and the run 1 % down, which is the per cent it changes for 1 % more of the constant; for pH, an SI and the like, how much it changes. A decay's A at the end, A0 e−kT, moves −kT per cent for each per cent of k. Two lines with the same equation and different constants, and a parameter with a value for each cell, are left out, since one value put in for all would move them by more than 1 %; so are an expression and nought, which a per cent does not move. The note says which. Each log K in turn does the same for every relation's and phase's logK, moved by log10 1.01 either way, so that its K is 1 % more and 1 % less, and ranks them on the same scale: the per cent a result changes for 1 % more of K. Calcite dissolving into water with nothing but its ions, Ca = √K, moves half a per cent for each per cent of K. Which equilibrium constants a result depends on is how the uncertainty of thermodynamic data is screened; both together rank the constants and the log K's in one list. Through the run shows when each matters: every run is read at the same 200 times by the solver itself, a hundred spread evenly and a hundred in the logarithm, and the eight constants that move the result most anywhere in the run are drawn as curves of the per cent it changes for 1 % more of each, against time — a decay's A, A0 e−kt, along −kt. Every species at the cell and every quantity but a reaction's rate and extent are kept through the run; those, too many in a network, at the end only.

Fitting to what was measured

Fit moves up to four numbers of the model until the run is as near as it can be to what <DATA> measured, as PhreePlot and Gibbs Studio fit PHREEQC. A number may be anything Sweeps can vary — a setting, a parameter, a species' start or what flows in — or a relation's or a phase's logK, or a number a reaction's line names: k, kf, or a constant of a rate law of your own, vmax = 2e-5. Each run is the text with the numbers put in, run with the panel's solver settings and read at exactly the times measured; a table in distance is read along the column, between the cells either side of each point.

The misfit is the sum of the squared residuals: for an amount the log10 of the run's value over the one measured, so that a factor of two counts the same at any size and a value at or below zero is left out; for pH, a saturation index and the like the difference. It is made smaller by Levenberg and Marquardt's method, each derivative from a run with one number moved a little; a number above zero is moved in its logarithm, which keeps it above zero, and a log K and the temperature as they are. The standard errors and correlations come from the derivatives at the end, the scatter from the residuals; the range of about 95 % is 1.96 standard errors either way, a factor for a number fitted in its logarithm. Fitted to data made by the model itself from known numbers, it finds them to the solver's tolerance; to noisy data where the answer can be worked out by hand, its value and standard error are the hand's to a part in a million. Put the fitted values in the model writes them into the text where they came from, to six figures.

The last of Show, the run against what was measured, one to one, needs no fit: each value <DATA> measured is set against the run's at the same place and time — the run on show read between its stored points and, along the column, between the cells either side, as the fit reads a table in distance — on equal axes with the line of one to one through them, amounts on log axes with the lines of twice and half dashed, pH, a saturation index and the like on axes of their own. Under it a table says, column by column, how far off the run is: the bias, the mean residual, which for an amount is shown as the factor the run is of what was measured on the whole; the spread, the residuals' root mean square; the sum of their squares; and the worst point. Then all the columns together, whose sum of squares is the fit's misfit. After a fit the fitted run is there too, as diamonds, at the times measured, beside the run as it was; a run kept beside is set against the same measurements, hollow, so that two models are judged by the same numbers.

Uncertainty

Uncertainty runs the model for draws of what is not known exactly, as Gibbs Studio's Monte Carlo does. Up to eight numbers — anything Sweeps can vary — are each given a distribution: normal, lognormal (a median, and the factor one standard deviation takes it times or over by), uniform, log-uniform or triangular. They are drawn by distributions.html's own sampling, by Latin hypercube, Sobol or simple random, each from a stream of its own so that the same seed gives the same draws. From the fit takes the last fit's numbers, each about its fitted value by its standard error, and draws them together with the fit's correlations through a Gaussian copula — the uncertainty of a calibrated model carried into what it predicts.

Every run is read at the same times, evenly over the run and evenly in the logarithm over its last six decades, so that the bands are taken across the runs at each of them: 5 to 95 % and 25 to 75 % of the runs, their median, the model as it is written dashed beside them, and what <DATA> measured at the cell. Along the column the bands are of each run's end. One value of each run — at the end, its largest or its smallest — is drawn as a histogram with its 5 %, median and 95 %, and Spearman's rank correlation of each number drawn with it says what matters: near 1 or −1 the value follows that number up or down, near 0 it does not.

A rank correlation sees only a value that goes one way with a number. What matters, by sensitivity indices reads the same runs three more ways, with nothing run again: each number's first-order Sobol' index, by EASI — the share of the value's variance the number explains alone; Borgonovo's δ — how far knowing the number moves the value's whole distribution, its tails as much as its middle, so that a number that matters only through another, or turns the value back, still shows; and PAWN, the median over ten slices of the number of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance between the value's distribution there and overall. Each has a 95 % interval: the index plus and minus 1.96 times its standard deviation over 100 bootstrap resamples of the runs, as SALib gives them, δ reduced for its bias as SALib reduces it. δ is read off the value's normal scores — its ranks put through the inverse of the normal distribution — which leave it as it is, δ being the same for any increasing transformation of the value (Borgonovo, Tarantola, Plischke and Morris, 2014), and keep it sound where most runs end alike, as a front that has come through makes them: off the value itself its densities turn into spikes. A dummy is drawn beside the numbers that no run used: its bars are how large chance alone makes an index with this many runs, and a number whose bars are no longer than the dummy's makes no difference that the runs can tell. The methods are Kompartment's ports of GlobalSensitivity.jl's EASI and δ and SALib's PAWN, which give their numbers for the same samples; the resamples and the dummy come from the seed, so that the same runs give the same indices. They want runs: twenty at the least, and a few hundred before the intervals narrow.

Two runs side by side

Change the model or its settings, run again, and the kept run is drawn beside the new one; beside it chooses which, of four kept at most, the oldest let go when a fifth is. A kept run outlives its model: another example, or a file opened since, is drawn beside it as well. Against distance, each profile is drawn again at the kept run's stored time nearest, dashed in the same colour along its own cells, a time past its end left out. Taking the run away with you

Export, the menu at the right end of a tab's bar, saves what the tab shows, in the formats that tab has. CSV writes what is ticked in the list, at the cell the chart is showing, a row per stored step, quantities and all; Excel writes the same as a workbook, the model text the run was made with on a second sheet. On Against distance and Gradient they write what the chart draws: a row per distance and a column per curve, or a row per cell and a column per time; on Composition each band, or each water's ions in equivalents, their shares and its type; on Diagrams each point's value or name; on Sweeps what is drawn, a row per value or per point; on Fit each point measured, the run there and its residual; on Uncertainty the percentiles at each time or cell, or each run's numbers and value. SVG and PNG save the chart itself, at the size it is drawn, for a report. HTML saves it as a page of its own, as live as it is here — zoom, pan, hover, a PNG of it — with the chart's note above it and the model text the run was made with under it; the PHREEQC tab's two charts save the same way. plotly.js, which draws it, goes into the file: the very build this page runs, read again under the hash the page loads it with, so the page opens in any browser without the internet. A dual-porosity model can have more cells than a file should hold datasets, and past four thousand of them the rock layers are left out — the root attribute matrix_written says so when that happens, and the water is always there.

PHREEQC in the page

PHREEQC does what this page does not. It has a thermodynamic database, activity coefficients, ion pairs, minerals and saturation, and it speciates a water from its analysis. This page instead integrates stiff networks of reactions, with transport, in one implicit system. The two are joined both ways here. A water goes from PHREEQC into the model, and a run goes back through PHREEQC to be read. PHREEQC itself runs in the page: the U.S. Geological Survey's program, compiled to run in the browser. Nothing is uploaded. It is fetched the first time it is used: 2.4 MB, 0.8 MB as it travels.

A water from PHREEQC

From PHREEQC… on the Model tab opens a panel that takes PHREEQC input. That is a SOLUTION, and anything PHREEQC should do to it first: EQUILIBRIUM_PHASES to put it at calcite and a CO2 pressure, a REACTION, a MIX. PHREEQC calculates it, and Write the model turns the water, as PHREEQC leaves it, into the model text:

  • every aqueous species above the floor, at PHREEQC's molality, in <SPECIES>;
  • every complexation and acid–base reaction among them, as the database writes it, in <EQUILIBRIUM>;
  • with Hold the valence states at one pe, the default, each species the database writes with electrons tied to the electron by that reaction, with the database's own log K (see Redox: the electron above); without it, no reaction with electrons, so that each valence state keeps its own total and electrons move only through the reactions you write.

Held at one pe, the water is the one PHREEQC's next step makes. PHREEQC keeps the valence states a SOLUTION is given, whatever its pe says — sulfate given, there is no sulfide in it, however reducing — and any reaction then puts every couple on one pe, the electrons kept, and forms the valence states it was given none of. Where the input ends on a SOLUTION as given, it is mixed with nothing else and that water written; the report says how far its pe moved. With the floor at 0 every species PHREEQC lists is written, even one it finds none of — PHREEQC counts nothing below 10−30 — at none and with the database's log K: oxygen in a reducing water, ferric iron's complexes. A water that oxygen will enter needs them.

The equilibria during the run says what keeps the relations on equilibrium once the run starts. Held exactly, the default, writes EQUILIBRATE = always, and each relation is an algebraic equation in every cell all through the run (see <EQUILIBRIUM> above). On equilibrium at the start only writes EQUILIBRATE = 1, and only the reactions you add move the species after that.

Held as fast reversible pairs writes EQUILIBRATE = 1 and gives each relation a kf, with the backward constant kb = kf/K. Near equilibrium a relation relaxes at kf times the product of its reactants times Σ ν²/c over its species, and that is solved for kf. Kinetic reactants in the input, a KINETICS with its rates in the database's or the input's RATES, are carried as reactions at a rate. Each reactant is a mineral at its -m to each kilogram of water, and its formula's dissolution — a phase's as the database's PHASES write it, or a chemical formula's, -formula CH2O, into the master species of its elements with H+, the water and the electrons — a reaction in <REACTIONS> at the rate its program comes to, the program written above it. A run through PHREEQC

The PHREEQC tab gives PHREEQC every stored water of the run, up to Times per cell of them in each cell. PHREEQC then speciates them with its own constants, activity model and ion pairs.

  • Each species the database has, under the same name or the renamed one, is counted into the element totals PHREEQC takes: HCO3- into C(4), H2 twice into H(0), (UO2)2(OH)2+2 twice into U(6).
  • The pH is walked until PHREEQC's molality of H+ is the run's [H+].
  • A mineral of the model, or an end member of a solid solution, is not part of the water: it is listed with the phases, its saturation index beside theirs. Nor is an exchanger, a surface or a species on either: each is looked at whole, below.
  • The model's electron, if it has one, is PHREEQC's: each water is given the pe the run has there, and the valence states are looked at against it, below.
  • Left out, and listed with their largest concentration: water; an e- that is not the electron, the hydrated electron of a radiolysis model; radicals and whatever else the database lacks.
  • The temperature is the one the model was written at, read from the top of its text, or 25 °C for a model that does not say. Where the model's TEMPERATURE goes with the cell or the time, each water is given the one it has in the run.
  • fill in from the water, off unless ticked, takes the temperature from the water in the panel on the Model tab. It also takes any element the model does not carry at all, such as a background electrolyte, held at its totals. A model written from a water carries every element already, so this is for one that leaves its counter-ions out. A water PHREEQC will not take is said, and left out.

What comes back is the pH as an activity and the saturation indices, drawn against time for one cell, or, under Show, against distance down the column at one of the times looked at, where a front's chemistry is a shape; and a CSV with every cell, the ionic strength, the charge balance and the activity of water. It also says:

  • which phases are supersaturated, and where and when — what would precipitate if the model let it;
  • how far the activity coefficients in the model's own equilibria moved along the run, in log K units. For a model with constants fixed at the start, that is how far they have gone wrong: a few hundredths is fine, and a tenth or more means a fresh water nearer the run. A model with ACTIVITY follows them itself, and then the tab says instead how close its own activity coefficients stay to PHREEQC's along the run, relation by relation. Under SIT PHREEQC's waters there are initial solutions, with A at a(H2O) = 1 (see SIT), and a salt water's coefficients are that far apart: 1.3×10−7 in log K at I = 2.7 and 25 °C, more when hot. PHREEQC's waters are put at the model's PRESSURE, and with <VOLUMES> the part of each log K that goes with the pressure is counted on both sides, PHREEQC's from its own molar volumes;
  • for each mineral of the model, the saturation index PHREEQC finds where the model has some of it, which holding it means is its target, and how far below saturation the water is where it has none;
  • for an exchanger of the model, how close the run's equivalent fractions are to the ones PHREEQC puts on it when it loads an exchanger of the run's capacity from the same water, species by species, at every water looked at;
  • for a surface of the model, how close the run's fractions of the sites are to the ones PHREEQC puts on it when it loads a surface with the run's sites from the same water, species by species, at every water looked at: with its double layer, the potential's area and grams given, where the model has a potential, and then how close the run's potential is to PHREEQC's as well; by CD-MUSIC, with its capacitances, where it has three, and then each of its potentials against PHREEQC's; without electrostatics where it has none;
  • for a gas phase of the model, how close each gas's partial pressure in the run — its fugacity, for a real gas — is to the pressure PHREEQC finds the same water holds it at, 10SI, wherever the run has any of it;
  • for a solid solution of the model, how close each member's activity in the run — its mole fraction times λ, or inside a miscibility gap the gap's edge's — is to the one PHREEQC finds the same water holds the pure member at, 10SI, wherever the run has any of it;
  • for the electron of the model, the pe over the waters, and how close the run's valence states are to the ones PHREEQC shares each element out to when it is given the water at that pe with the element as one total, as a part of the element;
  • a water far from neutral, which usually means the model leaves out a counter-ion, and so PHREEQC's ionic strength is too low.

A look at a run stays on the tab after the next run, saying that it is of the run before.

Inverse models through PHREEQC

Below the look through PHREEQC, Inverse models asks PHREEQC's INVERSE_MODELING which phases, dissolving or forming, make one water of the run out of another: from the water flowing in at the left, or a cell of the column, to another, each cell at start, end or a time such as 2 d — the stored point nearest it. Each water is given to PHREEQC as a look gives it, its element totals and its pe and temperature, the pH walked until PHREEQC's H+ is the run's. The phases are the database's, named as Calcite, Gypsum, CO2(g); one followed by dissolve or precipitate goes that way only, and with none named the model's own minerals and gases are taken. Every element the waters have, but hydrogen and oxygen, is balanced, each total allowed to move by the uncertainty, a fraction of it, as PHREEQC's -uncertainty; the water a mineral brings or takes, gypsum's two waters, is not counted (-mineral_water false), each water of a run being a kilogram of water as the run keeps it; ranges asks how far each transfer can go in any model the uncertainty allows, and minimal models only leaves out a model a smaller set of the phases does the work of.

Each model's mole transfers are drawn as bars, dissolving above zero and forming below, the ranges as whiskers, and listed with the adjustments PHREEQC made to the totals and its three measures of how far off it is: the sum of the residuals, the sum of each adjustment over its uncertainty, and the largest adjustment as a fraction of its total. The numbers are PHREEQC's, to the four figures it prints them to: it keeps its inverse models out of its selected output. A model found between a run's start and its end gives back what the run's own reactions did, to those figures — calcite dissolved at a rate for a day is that rate times a day. CSV saves every number of every model.

The Jacobian

Every rate law is differentiated symbolically with respect to each species it mentions. Nothing is differenced, so there is no step size to choose and no accuracy to lose. The chemistry is the same in every cell, so the matrix is one block per cell plus the transport terms, and those couple a species only to itself next door — which is why the state is ordered cell by cell rather than species by species: the matrix comes out banded instead of spread across its whole width.

The Jacobian tab draws the pattern of that matrix: the chemistry of each cell in the accent colour, the transport couplings to its two neighbours in the text colour, which is what puts the two thin diagonals either side of the blocks. In batch mode there is one block and it fills the picture. Nothing about the drawing is a guess — the worker folds the real pattern into a cell against itself and against each neighbour, which is the whole of it, and the caption says how much of each block is actually there.

Check Jacobian compares it against a central difference. Read the result with one thing in mind: an entry far smaller than the largest in its column cannot be measured that way at all — the change it makes is lost under the change the big ones make — so those are counted separately rather than called wrong.

Choosing a solver

NDF is the default and is the right first choice. Its BDF formulas switch, under Advanced settings, runs the same integrator with the κ terms off. The menu is Kompartment's, under each method's own name, and the canister radiolysis page shares it.

Auto starts on an explicit method — Tsit5, or Vern7 below a relative tolerance of 10−5, at a tenth of the tolerances — watches every step for stiffness as DifferentialEquations.jl's default does, and hands the rest of the run to the NDF once it finds it. A model that never turns stiff, A → B → C or the Brusselator, stays explicit and costs less than on the NDF; a transport column turns stiff in its first steps and costs what the NDF does. Rosenbrock 2-3 and Dormand–Prince 4-5 are Kompartment's own one-step methods: the first forms its Jacobian and factorises it every step — once a run, on a model whose Jacobian cannot change, such as a tracer column or a decay chain — and the second is explicit, for a model that is not stiff. The Julia ports are independent implementations of other methods: FBDF is the one to try on a large transport problem, RadauIIA5 when two others disagree, DefaultODEAlgorithm to compare with Julia's own default, and Tsit5 and Vern7 for a model that is not stiff.

Two settings come from Kompartment too. First step sets the step the run starts with, where the derivative at the start does not say what is about to happen; 0 lets the solver choose. Norm control, the NDF's, judges the error against the size of the whole solution rather than each species in each cell against its own, as MATLAB's NormControl does — looser wherever the species differ by orders of magnitude, so it is off.

Every one of them is available wherever the page is: a run normally happens on a background worker, and where there is no worker to be had — a page opened straight off the disk cannot start one — it happens on the page's own thread instead. The answer is the same; what you lose is the progress bar and the Stop button, because the page cannot repaint until the run is over. Serving the folder rather than opening the file gets both back: python3 -m http.server in it, then the address it prints.

If NDF will not get through a model, try FBDF before anything else. The kind of chemistry this page is for is hard on a corrector: with rate constants up to 1016 against concentrations of 10-9, the terms of a rate balance cancel over sixteen digits and the residual cannot be worked out to better than about a part in 107. A Newton correction that has reached that floor stops shrinking, which an ordinary convergence test reads as divergence. This page tells the solver to take such a correction when it is within half the tolerance and the Jacobian is the one for that point; without it, a reaction set of this kind can take millions of steps to cover a few seconds, and with it runs in a few thousand.

Where the built-in model came from

Water radiolysis and uranium dissolution at a spent-fuel surface: 127 reactions over 36 species, converted mechanically from the skbrtm example set. Radiolysis enters as a source of G × dose rate, with G in mol/J and the dose rate in Gy/s, so with a litre of water weighing a kilogram the product is mol/L/s and no conversion factor is needed. The dose rate is written as a constant on each radiolysis line, which holds it uniform across the film — the real one is not, it falls away over the range of an alpha particle, about 30 µm. To put that profile in, move it into a parameter and let every radiolysis line use it:

<PARAMETERS>
DOSE  all  0.64*exp(-x/3.0E-5)

then delete the DOSE = 0.64 from the ends of those lines, so that the name resolves to the parameter instead.
