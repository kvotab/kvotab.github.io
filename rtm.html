Reactive transport in the browser

Write a reaction set the way you would write it down, say whether it sits in a stirred batch or in a column with diffusion and advection, and this page builds the equations, differentiates them into an analytic sparse Jacobian and integrates them with the same stiff solvers as the canister radiolysis page. Nothing is uploaded: the whole thing runs here.

The button at the right end of the tabs gives the page the whole window, without the site's header and footer; the kvot mark at the left of the tabs then goes to the home page, and the switch beside the button changes between light and dark. The page remembers the choice.

The model text

Nine sections, of which two are needed: <SPECIES> and <REACTIONS>, and any number of named tables, <TABLE name>. Everything after a # is a comment. A rock matrix beside the column is a few more settings; see A rock matrix. Wherever a number goes it may be written as arithmetic on numbers — 365*86400, 1.33*10**12, 2/3 — and is worked out when the model compiles. Open… loads a model text from a file, and so does dropping the file anywhere on the page; either also reads skbrtm's database files, see Opening skbrtm and BRUM databases.

Colour the syntax, on the Model tab, paints comments, section headings, numbers, function names and the words that mean something where they stand — a setting and the words its value may take, fixed and D on a species line, k and r in a reaction, all and matrix in a parameter. It is a second copy of the text drawn underneath the box you are typing in, which is the only way a plain text area can be coloured while keeping its own undo, selection and caret; turn it off for a very large model, or if the colours are in the way. The choice is remembered between visits.

<SPECIES>

H2O    55.56   fixed
H+     1.0E-7  D=9.3E-9  left=1.0E-7
UO2                      # no number: starts at zero, does not move

A name, the concentration it starts at, and then any of: fixed to hold it there for ever, D= its diffusion coefficient in m²/s, and left= / right= the concentration at a Dirichlet face. A species with no D does not diffuse; but in a column with ADVECTION the water carries every species that is not fixed, D or no D. immobile keeps a species where it is whatever the column does, and mineral makes it a pure phase: see Minerals below, and for a mineral that is an end member of a solid solution Solid solutions. exchanger makes it the sites of an ion exchanger and exchange a species on them: see Ion exchange below. surface makes it a species on a surface, with sites= its sites, and potential the surface's potential, with area= and grams=: see Surface complexation below. electron makes it PHREEQC's electron, with pe= its pe: see Redox: the electron below. gas makes it a gas of a gas phase, and Tc=, Pc= and omega= a real one: see A gas phase below. With a rock matrix a species also takes De= (or Dm=), Kd= or Rm=, and Kdf=: see A rock matrix below. With ACTIVITY it takes z=, a= and b=: see Activity below; under ACTIVITY = sit and pitzer, z= alone.

fixed is a statement about the equation, not a nudge: that species' row in the right-hand side and in the Jacobian is set to exactly zero, so nothing — reaction, diffusion, advection, a Dirichlet face, an equilibrium — can move it. It still drives everything else, and is still consumed on paper by any reaction that consumes it; it simply is not replenished from anywhere the model has to account for. That is what a large water reservoir, a buffered pH or an imposed dose field is. Because the row is zero, the answer for such a species is the number you wrote, and that number is what the charts and the CSV show: a stiff step leaves round-off in it — about one part in 1013 on the built-in model over nine hundred steps — and that is arithmetic rather than chemistry, so it is written back out. If it is ever larger than round-off could explain, the run says so instead of hiding it.

A fixed species inside an <EQUILIBRIUM> is held there too, and the bookkeeping follows: fix the H+ of a water equilibrium and charge stops being conserved, because the buffer supplies whatever the relation calls for. What is left is one relation in one unknown, and OH- goes to K/[H+]. Fixing every species of a relation is refused, since then there is nothing left to satisfy it with.

A name may be a chemical formula: e-, H+, OH-, C2O4-2, UO2+2, U_site, and groups in round brackets, one level deep: Fe(OH)3, UO2(CO3)3-4. Square brackets are what a rate law puts round a name, so a name cannot contain those.

R= (or mass=, the same thing) is the coefficient on the left of the equation rather than the right: R dC/dt = …. A retardation factor for a sorbing species, a porosity, a water content — anything that divides everything happening to that species, transport and reactions alike. R=4 turns diffusion at D into diffusion at D/4 and a decay at k into one at k/4. It may be a plain number or the name of a parameter, in which case it varies from cell to cell. It has to be greater than zero: a zero would make that equation algebraic rather than differential, which is what <EQUILIBRIUM> is for.

<REACTIONS>

A + 2 B => C, k = 1.4E11                       # mass action: k[A][B]²
A <=> B, kf = 1.0, kb = 0.5                     # both ways
S => P, r = vmax*[S]/(km + [S]), vmax = 2, km = 0.1   # a rate law of your own
=> H2O2, k = 1.0E-8                            # nothing on the left is a source
OH- => , k = 0.1                               # nothing on the right is a sink

The equation comes first, then the rate, then any constants the rate uses. With k = you get mass action and the rate is that constant times each reactant raised to its coefficient. With r = you write the rate yourself and a concentration goes in brackets — [S] — so that a name like H+ is never read as arithmetic. A power's exponent may be anything that does not depend on a concentration: a number, arithmetic such as (2/3), a constant of the line or a parameter.

Fe+2 + 0.25 O2 => Fe(OH)3, r = (kr/kh)*[O2]*[Fe+2]*[OH-]^2, kr = 1.33*10**12, kh = 1.3*10**-3
A => B, r = k*c^(2/3)*(1 - [B]/cmax), k = 5e-13, c = [A], cmax = 1.8e-6

A constant may be arithmetic, and may use the constants named before it on its line — kf = 2, kb = kf/4. A constant written c = [A] is an alias: in the law, c is the concentration of A, exactly as if [A] had been written there. Both are how skbrtm writes its databases, and they mean here what they say. (skbrtm itself pastes each constant into the law as text, so it reads (kr/kh) above as 1.33*10**12/1.3*10**-3 — a million times less.)

Calcite => Ca+2 + CO3-2, r = k*A*(1 - SR(Calcite)), k = 1e-7, A = 0.5
=> X, r = kx*{H+}^0.5                     # an activity: gamma times the concentration
=> Y, r = ky*exp(-0.5*I)                  # I, the ionic strength of the cell
A => B, r = k25*exp(-Ea/8.314*(1/TK - 1/298.15))*[A], k25 = 1e-5, Ea = 6e4   # TK, the cell's temperature

A rate law may read more than concentrations. {H+} is the activity of H+, its activity coefficient times [H+], as ACTIVITY works it out (without ACTIVITY, the concentration), and {H2O} the water's when no species is called H2O. I is the ionic strength of the cell, unless a parameter of that name says otherwise, and TK and TC its temperature in kelvin and in °C (see The temperature below). SI(Calcite) is a phase's saturation index, log(IAP/K) in activities, and SR(Calcite) its saturation ratio, IAP/K. The phase is one of <PHASES> below, or a mineral with its relation in <EQUILIBRIUM>. So a mineral can dissolve and precipitate at a rate, as PHREEQC's RATES have it — k·A·(1 − SR), or any law of the saturation — with the amount a mineral species the reaction consumes. Each of these reads every ion of the cell through the ionic strength, and the Jacobian follows it there. A law that should stop when the mineral is gone says so, as (m/m0)^(2/3) does in PHREEQC's examples.

The time unit

A model has one time unit and everything in it is in that unit: the rate constants, the diffusivities, TEND, a velocity. TIME_UNIT says which, and it converts nothing — the solver integrates the numbers it is given. What the setting does is let the page say so: label the time axis, read 500 a in the Times box, print a travel time, head a CSV column. Without it everything is called seconds, which is what a model that says nothing means.

So a geosphere model written in years has half-lives in years, De in m²/a and a velocity in m/a, and writes TIME_UNIT = year; the radiolysis models are in seconds throughout and say nothing. Mixing the two inside one model is the one mistake this cannot catch, and never could: a diffusivity in m²/s beside a half-life in years is simply a different model from the one that was meant.

The two ends of the column

Four kinds of face, under the names the transport literature gives them. Which you want depends on what is on the other side: a reservoir, a wall, water arriving, or nothing at all.

dirichlet
first type		 The face is held at the concentration on the species' left= or right=. Diffusion sees that concentration half a cell away, and the flow, if there is any, carries it in. Right for a face against a large well-mixed reservoir. It sets nothing inside the column: every cell, the one touching the face included, starts where <SPECIES> and <INITIAL> put it.
neumann
second type		 Nothing crosses. A wall.
robin, cauchy
third type		 The flow carries left= in and nothing else crosses: the whole flux through the face is u·c. This is Danckwerts' inlet, and it is the one to use where the boundary is water arriving at a stated concentration rather than a reservoir held at one. It needs a VELOCITY pointing inwards; the page says so if there is none.
free, outflow Zero gradient: no diffusion across the face, and the flow takes what it takes. Danckwerts' outlet, and the companion of robin at the other end. It asks for nothing to be known about what is beyond the face, which at an outlet is the point.
semi-infinite
right only		 The column goes on past its end, and the end is a plane inside it that the flow and the dispersion both cross: what leaves is the whole flux, and the concentration falls to zero only infinitely far downstream. The outlet of the analytical far-field models, FARF31's among them, and of Kompartment's far-field path. EXTRA_CELLS cells as wide as the last stand for the column beyond, the last of them closed by carrying the gradient on through it. It needs the flow to run to the right.

The difference between the first and third types is worth knowing, because it is a common way to get an inlet wrong. A Dirichlet face adds a diffusive flux D(cb − C)/d on top of what the flow brings, so more enters than the water carries, and the column's mass balance does not close: measured on the test column in resources/tests/rtm/, 6.7 % more goes in than u(cin − cout) accounts for. With robin and free the same column balances to one part in 1015, and its profile matches the flux-type closed solution of van Genuchten and Alves, converging on it by a factor of four each time the cells are doubled.

As arithmetic, free and neumann are the same face — the advective outflow applies at every cell whatever the far boundary says, so a Neumann outlet already lets the flow leave. They are separate words because they say different things and are wrong in different ways, and the page can only tell you which is wrong if it knows which you meant.

How many cells a semi-infinite end needs past it is a matter of how far upstream the dispersion reaches. It dies away by D/(D + uΔx) per cell, which for a Peclet number and no D of a species' own is (2N − Pe)/(2N + Pe), and unless EXTRA_CELLS says otherwise they are the fewest that bring that under a tenth: five for twenty cells at Pe 10, as Kompartment counts them, and none where the grid's own dispersion is all there is. They are the column going on, so a line of <PARAMETERS> or <INITIAL> that reaches the last cell reaches them too, and a rock matrix is behind them as well. The charts leave them out.

A Danckwerts outlet, free, closes the column at its end instead, and lets out 4a/(1 + a)² of what the semi-infinite column does at a frequency where a = √(1 + 4twg/Pe). Against FARF31's exact transfer function, the far-field path below is 2.4 % out where most of a release is with a semi-infinite end, and 6.8 % with a free one. More cells bring the first down by four per doubling, to 0.17 % on 160, and make the second worse, 9.9 % on 160: that column closes on Danckwerts' answer, which is not FARF31's.

What crosses an end. On Against time the Cell picker ends with flux through the left end and through the right end: what crosses the plane at 0 or at LENGTH, per m² of the water's cross-section and positive to the right, in concentration times metres per unit of time. At an outlet that is the release — advection alone at a free end, advection and dispersion at a semi-infinite one. It is worked out from the stored run with exactly the terms the equations put through that face, so what the column gains is what crosses its left end less what crosses its right, to round-off.

A rock matrix: dual porosity

Water flows along a fracture; the rock beside it is stagnant, porous, and open to diffusion. A species carried along the fracture leaks sideways into the rock's pore water, sorbs there, and comes back out long after the water that brought it has gone — which is what holds a radionuclide up in a geosphere by orders of magnitude more than the water's own travel time. Say anything about the rock and every cell of the column gets a chain of stagnant cells behind it, twelve unless MATRIX_CELLS says otherwise (0 for none), reaching MATRIX_DEPTH metres into the rock. A far-field path is written the way the SKB reports describe one, and here as Kompartment's far-field block starts one:

<SETTINGS>
MODE = transport
CELLS = 20                  # along the path
LENGTH = 100                # m; any length: TRAVEL_TIME sets the flow
TRAVEL_TIME = 100           # the water's travel time t_w, in the time unit
PECLET = 10
LEFT = robin                # water arriving at left= per m3
RIGHT = semi-infinite       # the rock goes on past the release point
MATRIX_DEPTH = 12.5         # m into the rock
MATRIX_POROSITY = 0.0018
MATRIX_DENSITY = 2700       # kg/m3, the default
TRANSPORT_RESISTANCE = 1E5  # F = a_w t_w: 1000 m2 of wall per m3 of water here
TIME_UNIT = year

<SPECIES>
Cs  0  left=1  De=1E-4  Kd=0.01    # De in m2/a, Kd in m3/kg

Each species has its De= (or Dm=, the same thing), the effective diffusivity in the rock, and a capacity there: Kd=, the partition coefficient, makes it εm + ρmKd with MATRIX_POROSITY and MATRIX_DENSITY, and Rm= gives it outright and may name a parameter, so that it varies from cell to cell. With neither the species has the porosity alone, which is one that does not sorb; with no De it never enters the rock. Kdf= is sorption on the fracture coating, which retards the species in the water by R = 1 + Kd,faw; R= says it outright.

The flow-wetted surface aw, the area of fracture wall a cubic metre of flowing water touches, is given one of three ways: WETTED_SURFACE itself, in m²/m³; APERTURE (2b) for a fracture of two walls, as 2/aperture; or TRANSPORT_RESISTANCE, the F of the SKB reports, as F/tw. Two of them given have to agree. TRAVEL_TIME gives the flow as the water's travel time; without it tw is LENGTH over the pore-water speed. A unit release into the path is a robin left end with left at one over the pore-water speed, or a source => X, k = S with S 0 1/Δx fracture in <PARAMETERS>; the release out is what crosses the right end.

The layers grow geometrically with depth, because the gradient is steepest at the wall and all but flat far in, and MATRIX_GRID says how:

matched
the default		 Kompartment's far-field layers. Each node sits where the layers' exchange with the fracture matches exact diffusion into the rock — spaced by the geometric mean of the two layers it joins rather than at their centres — and the first layer is worked out from the path: a tenth of the depth diffusion reaches at the fastest change that still gets through it (where the path's transfer function has fallen to e−25), or at a species' own decay where that is faster. One grid for every species, so the shallowest decides. A species' decay is read off its first-order on = inventory reactions. With no flow and no decay to size it by, the first layer is the reference one.
reference FARFCOMP's layers: a first layer that makes them grow by e, nodes at their centres, the thicknesses bit for bit those of SKB's own implementation. At a ratio of e nodes at the centres understate what the rock takes up by 5.8 %.

MATRIX_FIRST sets the first layer instead, whichever the grid. The far face of the last layer is closed: the depth is a depth, not a boundary. Layers that grow by more than 2.5 are coarse at depth, and the page then says how many would keep the growth to 2. On the U-238 chain among the examples, Po-210's half-life of 0.38 years sizes the first layer at 5 µm: twelve layers to 4.5 m grow by 3.4 and put the releases 4 % out, twenty grow by 2.0 and are within 0.2 %.

Concentrations are per unit pore water everywhere, and so are the rate laws. A cubic metre of rock holds only εm of water, so a rate r per unit water changes the rock's inventory by εmr, and it is the inventory, RmC, that the rock's equation is written for: Rm dC/dt = εmr + diffusion. That is right for chemistry in the water and wrong for one thing: radioactive decay, which does not ask where an atom sits, sorbed or dissolved. Write such a rate with on = inventory and it removes the same share of everything in the cell, and makes as much of what is on the right: a daughter born of a parent that sorbs hard is born as many atoms as the parent lost, and then takes up its own share between water and rock, as Kompartment and FARF31 have it. Such a reaction has one species on the left, or none.

Cs => , k = 7.3E-10, on = inventory     # decay: of everything, sorbed included
U234 => Th230, k = 2.8234E-6, on = inventory   # a chain: Th-230 from all of the U-234
Cs + X => Y, k = 1.2E-4                   # chemistry: in the water, on = water is the default

Told it acts on the water alone, decay in a sorbing rock runs at εm/Rm of its real rate; on the reference case below that is the difference between a release fraction of 0.116 and 0.717.

A line in <INITIAL> or <PARAMETERS> names cells of the column. Add matrix after it to mean the rock behind those cells instead, or fracture to mean the flowing water alone. A parameter with neither applies to both — right for a temperature, wrong for a source into the water, which would otherwise also feed every layer of rock behind that cell. In a parameter's expression j is the layer (0 in the fracture) and xm the depth to its centre in metres.

How fine. With matched layers, twelve do as well as twenty: what is left is the cells along the path, and they converge on the exact answer at second order. On the path above, Ra-226 (De=1E-4 Kd=4.5E-4, decaying with its half-life of 1600 years, as in the far-field example) arrives at 2.2×10−5 of a steady release on 20 cells, where FARF31's exact answer is 1.72×10−5; 40 cells give 1.84×10−5 and 80 give 1.75×10−5. A strongly held-back nuclide wants more cells than a tracer does.

What was checked. Kompartment's far-field path, from its own code, on five paths: its matched layers bit for bit, its count of cells past the release point, every entry of its transport matrix and of its chain's ingrowth to one part in 1015, and its release, read at the same plane with the same weights. The model's transfer function against FARF31's exact one, from far below the path's own frequencies to where it has fallen to e−15. With MATRIX_GRID = reference, the layer thicknesses and every rate against SKB's own implementation of TR-19-06's formulation, in five cases including twenty layers from 4×10⁻⁸ m, to one part in 1014, and two of its release curves, one with sorption and decay, to 2×10-9 and 2×10-10 at a tolerance of 10-9. Neretnieks' closed solution for a fracture beside a deep matrix, converging at the upwind scheme's first order as the fracture is refined; a closed box that conserves mass to 10-14 and shares it between water and rock exactly by capacity; and a decay chain that keeps its atoms in water and rock alike.

On the charts, a Layer picker beside the cell chooses the fracture or a rock layer for the time chart and the gradient, and Against distance can be drawn across the fracture at one layer or into the rock at one cell, the fracture at depth zero.

A release history

A source that follows the clock is a reaction with nothing on the left whose rate reads a table at the time, t: what a near field releases into a far-field path, year by year, as another code worked it out or as a report tabulates it.

<TABLE release>
t           U238      U234          # the first row names the columns; t in years
0           0         0
1000        2.0E-3    1.5E-3
1.0E4       1.1E-3    9.0E-4
1.0E5       0         0

<PARAMETERS>
IN  all  0
IN  0    1/w  fracture              # into the first cell: one per m2 of water's cross-section

<REACTIONS>
=> U238, r = IN*release(t, U238)
=> U234, r = IN*release(t, U234)

IN turns a release per m² of the water's cross-section into one per m³ of the first cell's water; what crosses the right end, on Against time, is then per m² too, and the two compare directly. The time is in the model's own unit, and a table is held at its last row beyond it: end a history with zeros if it ends. The argument may be any arithmetic on t and the line's constants — release(t - 1000) to start it a thousand years late, or release(t/3.156e7) to read a history in years in a model in seconds.

The run is started again at every corner of such a table — every row, moved and scaled with the argument when that is a straight line in t. A stiff solver that has grown its steps over a quiet stretch would otherwise step clean over a short pulse and never see it: a release of ten years in five thousand comes out as nothing at all without the restarts, and whole with them. Kompartment does the same at its switch times. The panel says how many corners there are, and the status line how many the run was started at; a history of a few hundred rows costs a few thousand steps.

Through Kompartment's default far-field path, a history that rises, holds and falls over twenty thousand years comes out as FARF31 convolves it with the path's exact response, to the accuracy of the cells along the path: for Ra-226, which the rock holds back by five orders, the peak is 30 % high on 20 cells, 7 % on 40 and 1.8 % on 80.

What may go in an expression

The same arithmetic in a rate law, a constant and a parameter: + - * /, brackets, and ^ or ** for a power. Numbers may be written 1.4E11 or 1.4e11. The functions are:

exp(a)ea.
log(a), ln(a)Natural logarithm — log means the natural one here, as it does in FACSIMILE.
log10(a)Base ten.
sqrt(a)Square root.
abs(a), sign(a)Magnitude and sign.
step(a)1 where a is above zero, 0 elsewhere.
ramp(a)a where it is above zero, 0 elsewhere — max(a, 0) by another name.
min(a,b), max(a,b), pow(a,b)In <PARAMETERS> only; see below.

Three of those are barred from a rate law, and the reason is the Jacobian rather than taste. min and max have a derivative that jumps where their two arguments cross, so an analytic Jacobian would be wrong on one side of the crossing; pow(a,b) differentiates to something containing log a, which is undefined at a zero concentration — which is exactly where a rate law gets evaluated. A power whose exponent does not depend on a concentration — [A]^2, [A]^(2/3), [A]^n with n a constant or a parameter — has neither problem and is what to write. <PARAMETERS> is worked out once when the model compiles and never differentiated, so all three may be used there.

step and ramp are allowed, with the derivative of the kink taken as zero: they are flat everywhere they are differentiable, and a stiff solver steps over the corner rather than through it. A rate that switches hard is still worth avoiding where a smooth one will do.

Which names are in scope depends on where the expression is. A rate law may use the concentrations it writes in brackets, the constants named on its own line, any parameter, t, the time in the model's own unit, and a table read at that time — see A release history below; a constant on the reaction line wins if a parameter has the same name, and no parameter may be called t. A parameter, and a starting value in <INITIAL>, may use x, the cell centre in metres, w, the cell's width, xl and xr, its two faces, i, the cell index, the tables, and any parameter named above it.

A surface

A dissolving surface — sites, a coating, a reacting film — is written as species that live in the first cell, stated per m² of surface and spread through that cell: U_site 0 2.1e-4/w/1000 in <INITIAL>, with no D so that they stay put. The amount per m² is then right on any grid.

What the chemistry does there is another matter. A rate law is written in concentrations, so a reaction between two surface species goes as the square of something divided by the cell's width, and when that width comes from the grid, refining the grid moves the answer: in skbrtm's fuel-dissolution example the dissolved uranium per m² halves each time the cells are halved. SURFACE_LAYER pins the first cell's thickness, making it a property of the model — a reaction zone, as the batch model's 40 µm alpha layer is — and lays the rest of the column out behind it. Refine the grid then and the surface does not change.

This is how a dose rate that dies away from a surface is written, or a porosity that changes down a column, or a rate constant that follows a temperature profile. A rate law may use a parameter by name, exactly as it uses a constant declared on its own line; a constant on the reaction line wins if both have the same name.

A relation that has to hold rather than a rate that has to be integrated: ∏[C]ν = K, with logK its base-ten logarithm, which is the form a database gives. dH, the enthalpy of reaction in kJ/mol, or analytic, an analytic expression, makes the log K go with the temperature, as a PHREEQC database's delta_h and -analytic do: see The temperature below. An empty left-hand side means the reaction has no reactant, as it does for water dissociating. H2O, when it is not one of the species, is the water itself, and a relation may be written with it as a database writes it: H2O <=> H+ + OH-, CO2 + H2O <=> H+ + HCO3-. It is never a concentration. Its activity is 1, which leaves the relation as it would be without it, unless ACTIVITY says otherwise.

With EQUILIBRATE = 1 the starting concentrations are put on these relations before the run: a damped Newton iteration in ln C, run separately in every cell, over the species the relations mention and no others — anything they do not touch is left exactly where it was put, zero included. What it may not change is worked out from the relations themselves — the left null space of their stoichiometry, which gives the conserved totals without the page needing a table of elements. So writing H+ at 10-3 and OH- at 10-3 gives 10-7 of each, and the difference between them, which is a charge, is exactly what it was.

During the run a relation can be kept in two ways. Give it a kf and it becomes a fast reversible pair, with kb = kf/K: two reactions that pull it back to equilibrium whenever something moves it off. A kf a few orders above the fastest rate in the model holds the relation to within the solver's tolerance, and making it enormous only costs steps. But a pair always lags a little where the water changes fast, as it does in the first cell of a column taking a new water.

With EQUILIBRATE = always the relations given no kf are held exactly instead. In every cell each is then an algebraic equation, Σ ν ln C = ln K, and the species they move are no longer integrated one by one. What is integrated in their place are the totals the relations conserve, the same totals the start is speciated with: a reaction or a flux between cells changes a total as it changes the species in it, and the species are worked out from the totals at every step. That is a speciation in every cell, solved together with the reactions and the transport in one implicit system. The relations then hold to rounding at every stored point, and what they conserve is conserved to rounding. A species with an R counts in a total R times, so what is conserved is the inventory. A relation with a kf stays a fast pair beside the held ones, and a fixed species in a held relation must be above zero in every cell.

Such a system has algebraic equations in it, which only the NDF solves: Auto hands it to the NDF, and the other methods decline it. Each held species is solved for as ln C, and an error of rtol in ln C is a relative error of rtol in C, so the tolerance means for these species what it means for the others.

With ACTIVITY the relations of <EQUILIBRIUM> hold between activities, Σ ν log (γC) = log K, and each logK is then the thermodynamic constant a database gives rather than one made for a particular water. Every activity coefficient γ follows the ionic strength of its cell, I = ½ Σ z²C over all the cell's species, as the run goes on:

  • an ion, by Davies: log γ = −A z² (√I/(1 + √I) − 0.3 I);
  • under debye-huckel, an ion given a=, by the WATEQ Debye–Hückel form: log γ = −A z² √I/(1 + B a √I) + b I, with a= in ångström and b= in kg/mol, a database's -gamma;
  • a neutral species: log γ = b I, with b 0.1 unless b= gives it;
  • a species named H2O: γ = 1.

That is PHREEQC's rule, and on the same water the page's activity coefficients are PHREEQC's to rounding: 6×10−16 in log γ from a dilute water to 3 mol/kgw of NaCl, and from 25 to 150 °C. A and B come from each cell's temperature, TEMPERATURE, as PHREEQC works them out, from the dielectric constant and the density of water; from 0 to 200 °C they are PHREEQC's to nine digits. The water's activity is 1 − 0.017 ΣC, PHREEQC's too, and as in PHREEQC it moves A and B a little: PHREEQC takes the density of water at the vapour pressure times the water's activity, Raoult's law, and above 100 °C the water at that pressure, so a salty or hot water's A and B are its own. (That part of them is left out of the Jacobian, where it is a part in 105 of a coefficient's derivative at most.) A charge is read from the end of the name, H+ 1 and SO4-2 −2, and z= gives it where the name should not: a surface site is no ion in the water, and z=0 b=0 leaves it out of the ionic strength with γ = 1. Concentrations are read as molalities.

The activities act on the relations, wherever the page uses them: the speciation of the start, the relations held throughout the run, and the fast pairs, whose backward constant follows the ionic strength so that each pair relaxes to its K in activities. A rate law in <REACTIONS> stays in concentrations. A relation then reads every ion in its cell, through the ionic strength, so the Jacobian is fuller within a cell, and a little more work.

Redox as PHREEQC has it. electron makes a species PHREEQC's electron, e- in a database's reactions. It is no amount: its activity, 10−pe, is solved for in every cell, it counts in no total, and what the page shows for it is that activity. It never moves, is in no ionic strength and no water, and takes no D, R, rock-matrix properties or z=. A model has one. In a radiolysis model e- is the hydrated electron, a species like any other: the word on the line is what makes the difference.

A couple is tied to the electron by a relation, written as the database writes it, and every couple tied to it is in equilibrium with every other at one pe. The totals the relations conserve then include the electrons the couples hold between them, so a reaction in <REACTIONS> that moves electrons — oxygen arriving, iron oxidised at a rate — moves every couple together, and where the last of a reductant has gone the pe jumps as the oxidant builds up. A couple not tied to it keeps its valence states apart, as PHREEQC's decoupled elements do. The electron itself is in no reaction and no fast pair: redox at a rate is written between species, Fe+2 + 0.25 O2 + H+ => Fe+3 + 0.5 H2O, and a rate law may read the electron's activity as [e-]. A phase that dissolves with electrons, such as pyrite, is a mineral with the electron in its relation.

Given pe=, or an activity, the start is speciated at that pe first, as PHREEQC's SOLUTION is: each element tied to it is shared out between its valence states as that pe has it, which is how one given none of a state gets some. Given neither, the pe is the water's own, from the valence states as written, and EQUILIBRATE is needed. fixed holds the pe all through the run, a redox buffer.

The relations with the electron hold all through the run under EQUILIBRATE = always, and at the start only otherwise. Held, a model with the electron is solved a little differently from one without: each cell's species are the speciation of its totals, found at every evaluation by a damped Newton iteration of its own in the cell's components, as PHREEQC speciates a water in its master species, and only the totals are held to the tolerance. That is what carries a run through the moment no couple buffers the pe — the last pyrite gone, the oxygen coming in not yet built up — where the pe moves by ten while what is conserved moves by less than the arithmetic can tell.

PHREEQC's GAS_PHASE. gas makes a species a gas of the cell's gas phase: its amount is the moles of it in the gas to each kilogram of water, it stays in its cell, as PHREEQC's TRANSPORT leaves a gas phase, and it counts in the totals as anything else does. Each gas is in a relation that dissolves it, written as PHREEQC's PHASES write it, and a gas's activity is its partial pressure in atm. It takes no D, fixed, z=, a= or b=.

In a fixed volume, GAS_VOLUME, a gas's partial pressure is n R T/V, R PHREEQC's 0.0820597 L·atm/(K·mol) and T the cell's temperature. The gas phase is simply there, however little of a gas is in it, and its relations hold as any others do. At a fixed total pressure, GAS_PRESSURE, the gas is a bubble, and a gas's partial pressure is its share of the bubble times the total. The bubble is there only while the water holds its gases at that total between them. While it is there, its relations say that the pressures the water holds its gases at add up to the total, and that each gas's share of the bubble is its share of those pressures; while it is not, every gas's amount is zero. It forms where the water comes to the total and dissolves where its last gas goes, and the run stops there and goes on, as at a mineral's (see Minerals). A gas at a fixed pressure is in one relation, held by EQUILIBRATE and with no kf; in a fixed volume a relation with a gas may be a fast pair.

CO2(g)   0   gas   Tc=304.2   Pc=72.86   omega=0.225

Given its critical temperature in K and pressure in atm, and its acentric factor, a gas is a real one, held by Peng and Robinson's equation of state as PHREEQC holds a gas the database gives them: its activity is its fugacity, φ times its partial pressure. In a fixed volume the pressure is the equation's at the molar volume V/N, N the gases' amounts together; at a fixed pressure the molar volume is the root PHREEQC takes. φ is PHREEQC's, term for term, the equation's a for a pair of gases taken 1 − k times where <GAS_PAIRS> gives the pair a k. A gas phase is all real gases or all ideal, as in PHREEQC. Not carried: where the cubic has three roots — CO2 below its critical temperature, dense enough to condense — PHREEQC looks past the turning point of the pressure, and the page does not.

A bubble at a fixed pressure puts the water at that pressure, as PHREEQC does: the Debye–Hückel A and B and a double layer's dielectric constant are worked out there. Otherwise the water is at PRESSURE, 1 atm unless given. PHREEQC puts a water under a fixed volume of gas at the gas's pressure too, and follows it as the gas phase fills or empties, moving A and B and, where the database gives the species molar volumes, its log K; the page holds both where the run starts. For CO2 that is about 6×10−4 in log K for each atmosphere the pressure moves.

PHREEQC's SOLID_SOLUTIONS. A solid solution's end members are minerals (see Minerals), each in the one relation that dissolves it with the pure phase's log K, and each of one solid solution only. In it a member's activity is not 1 but its mole fraction x in the solid solution times λ: the members' amounts are its composition, and their relations hold the water at it. In an ideal solid solution λ is 1, and so it is in one of three members or more. Two members may be given Guggenheim's a0 and a1, dimensionless, as PHREEQC's -Gugg_nondimensional gives them, or g0 and g1 in kJ/mol, its -Gugg_kJ, and then, as PHREEQC writes them,

ln λ1 = x2² (a0 − a1 (3 − 4 x2))
ln λ2 = x1² (a0 + a1 (4 x2 − 1))

x2 the second member's mole fraction. They are given at 25 °C, or at tc=; at any other temperature a0 and a1 go as 1/T, their energies a R T held, as in PHREEQC.

A solid solution is there or not as a whole. It is there while the water holds it at saturation — each member's IAP/K over its λ, at the composition the water gives the solid, adding up to 1, which for two members is Lippmann's total solubility product — and each member's amount is then its share of what the totals leave. With EQUILIBRATE the start is put on it as PHREEQC puts a water on its SOLID_SOLUTIONS: where the water is supersaturated in it, it precipitates, at the composition the water then gives it; where there is some of it and the water is undersaturated, it dissolves until the water is saturated or none is left. With EQUILIBRATE = always that goes on through the run, as for a mineral: where the last of it dissolves the run stops at that instant, and every member's amount is held at zero; where the water becomes supersaturated in it again the run stops there and it precipitates. The status names it: the solid solution CaSrCO3 precipitates. A member counts in the totals, and under ACTIVITY its relation reads every ion of its cell, as a mineral's does.

Where a0 and a1 are large enough, the two members do not mix in every proportion. The page finds the miscibility gap at each cell's temperature as PHREEQC's ss_prep does, step for step: none above the solid solution's critical temperature, and below it the spinodal and the two compositions the gap lies between, where PHREEQC's search stops, at 10−6 in the activities. Where TEMPERATURE goes with time, the page then takes the two compositions on to round-off: PHREEQC's stopping point moves in jumps of up to 10−6 as the temperature moves, and a run held at the gap's edge cannot step across a jump. A composition inside the gap is two solids, one at each of them: the water is held at the activities the gap's edge gives each member, as PHREEQC holds it, and the amounts move between them. Where the composition comes to the gap, from either side, or leaves it, the run stops there and goes on, and the status says so: CaSrCO3 comes to its miscibility gap. Aragonite and strontianite, with Plummer and Busenberg's a0 = 3.43 and a1 = −1.82 as in PHREEQC's tenth example, have one between strontianite fractions of 0.0048 and 0.858 at 25 °C.

A relation's log K goes with temperature as PHREEQC's does, by its k_calc, T in kelvin:

log K = logK − dH (298.15 − T)/(ln 10 · R T · 298.15)
        + A1 + A2 T + A3/T + A4 log10 T + A5/T² + A6 T²

logK is its log K at 25 °C, dH its enthalpy of reaction in kJ/mol, held constant (van 't Hoff's equation), analytic = A1 A2 A3 A4 A5 A6 its analytic expression, up to six terms and the rest zero, and R PHREEQC's 0.00831470 kJ/(mol K). An analytic expression with a term not zero replaces logK and dH, as PHREEQC's databases have it: one may give all three, and the expression is the relation's log K. A relation with logK alone has it at every temperature. So does a phase of <PHASES>.

TEMPERATURE is a number of degrees, or an expression of the parameters and the clock: a parameter, TEMPERATURE = Tc with Tc all 10 + 3*i, for a temperature that differs from cell to cell; a table read at the clock, heat(t), for one that goes with time, the run started again wherever the table turns; or both, Tc + rise(t). Each cell's temperature sets, there and then, every relation's log K, the Debye–Hückel A and B of ACTIVITY (and SIT's), a gas's R T in a fixed volume and Peng and Robinson's terms for a real gas, a double layer's √(8000 R T ε ε₀) and its dielectric constant, a binary solid solution's a0, a1 and miscibility gap, Pitzer's parameters and Aφ, a fast pair's backward constant kf/K, and a saturation index in a rate law. A rate law reads it as TK, in kelvin, and TC, in °C: k25*exp(-Ea/8.314*(1/TK - 1/298.15)) is Arrhenius's law. It is worked out for 0 to 350 °C, as the water's A and B are, and a temperature outside that stops the run and says where.

A held relation then holds at the temperature of its cell at each instant: a water heated along a table is, at every stored time, the speciation of its totals at that time's temperature, and a mineral precipitates or dissolves as its solubility moves. Where a heated water goes over from gypsum to anhydrite, the run stops at the temperature where PHREEQC's saturation index of anhydrite crosses zero — 54.38 °C for a dilute water at calcite saturation — and goes on with anhydrite (see Minerals above).

The temperature is what TEMPERATURE says it is, cell by cell and time by time; it is not carried by the water or conducted through the rock. PHREEQC's TRANSPORT moves a solution's temperature with it unless told to hold each cell's, -thermal_diffusion 1e30 0, which is the page's column. Not carried with the temperature: diffusion coefficients, which stay as given; SIT's interaction coefficients, which stay as written (sit.dat gives none that go with temperature); and PHREEQC's pressure term in log K, which comes in above one atmosphere, from molar volumes the page does not carry.

Opening skbrtm and BRUM databases

Open… takes skbrtm's database files as well as a model text: select any of reaction.in, solutions.in, diffusion.in, the sourceterm and doserate files, and the Python script that runs them — or drop them on the page, or drop an example's whole folder, script and databases together; what is in it besides those is passed over. The script says which files are read, in what order, and for how long; without it every database selected is read and TEND is one second, to be set by hand. The files are read as skbrtm reads them — a SOLUTION sets every species in its cells, a species the DIFFUSION block does not name diffuses at 10-9 m²/s, names are the same whatever their case — and become one model text, with rate constants per second as skbrtm integrates them.

Where skbrtm would read the text differently from what it says, the model follows the text and the difference is written at the top of it. Every rate law is read both ways, and one whose arguments skbrtm pastes in so that the value changes is named, with the factor. A Dirichlet end holds the face here, where skbrtm holds the first cell; a REGRESSION becomes a <TABLE> of its own data, where skbrtm fits a function to it; a constant species becomes fixed, in the cells its SOLUTION names; and a lone cell_id means that cell, where skbrtm puts it in cell 0.

What it actually solves

For every species s in every cell i:

dC/dt = (what the reactions make and consume in cell i)
      + (diffusion across the two faces of cell i)
      + (advection in from upwind)

Diffusion is a finite-volume difference: the flux between two cells over the distance between their centres, divided by the width of the cell it lands in. On an even grid that is the textbook D/Δx²; on an uneven one it stays conservative, which the textbook form does not. Measured on a closed box, mass is held to about one part in 1015, and against the analytic erfc profile the error falls by four each time the cells are doubled — second order, as it should be.

Advection is upwind: a face carries the concentration of the cell upwind of it. That is stable at any step and never makes a new peak or trough, but it spreads a front by v·Δx/2 whatever the model says, as if it had a Peclet number of twice the cells. ADVECTION_SCHEME = limited adds to each face the slope of the cell upwind of it, times the distance to the face, the slope an average of the gradients on either side, 2a²b²(a+b)/(a²+b²)², and none where they differ in sign. That is second order where the profile is smooth, and upwind at a peak or a trough, so nothing goes below zero; as a limiter it lies inside the TVD region, and unlike van Albada's it starts from zero with zero slope, so the Jacobian is smooth and one a difference of the state can check. The first and last cells stay upwind, so what crosses the ends is as upwinding has it, and with PECLET nothing is taken back for the scheme. On a front at a Peclet number of 400, the largest error against the closed solution is 8 % on 40 cells and 0.7 % on 160, where upwinding's is 19 % and 2.9 %; it costs more steps, the Jacobian moving with the profile.

Diffusion that keeps the charge

Each species diffuses at its own D. That is right for a neutral molecule and wrong for ions whose coefficients differ: H+ diffuses more than four times as fast as Cl−, so acid spreading into water runs ahead of its chloride and the water between them is charged, which real water does not allow. DIFFUSION = nernst-planck couples the ions so that no current flows: across each face between two cells an ion moves at

J_k = −D_k ∇C_k + D_k z_k C_k (Σ z_j D_j ∇C_j) / (Σ z_j² D_j C_j)

the second term the pull of the diffusion potential, which makes the charges' fluxes cancel. A fast ion drags its slow partner along and is held back by it: HCl spreading into water does so at 2·DHDCl/(DH + DCl), 3.3×10−9 m²/s, and the page matches the closed solution for that to 2×10−5 of the step, the water neutral to 10−13, where without it the chloride lags the acid by a sixth of the step. Where every coefficient is the same the term is nothing in a neutral water. The charge comes from the name, z= where it should not; a PECLET dispersion moves every species alike and is left as it is. It acts between the cells of the column: a Dirichlet end and the rock matrix keep each species on its own, and the gradients are of concentrations, the activity coefficients' own gradients left out.

Reading the charts

The time chart draws the species ticked in the list above it, at one cell, or what crosses one end of the column (see The two ends of the column). Against distance draws one species across all the cells, at several times. Which times is up to you: the Times field takes a list — 0, 1 h, 30 d, 500 y, or bare numbers in the model's own unit — and leaving it empty spreads eight over the run.

Gradient puts the whole run in one picture: time across, distance up, concentration as colour. Where Against distance shows a handful of moments, this shows every stored one, so a front's arrival and how far it gets are a shape rather than a set of curves to compare. Distance runs up the axis from zero at the origin, so the left-hand face — the surface the chemistry happens at — is along the bottom.

The bands across it are the cells: with twenty of them the picture has twenty rows and says so. Smooth interpolates between them, which looks better and claims a resolution the run does not have. log concentration plots log10, leaving anything at or below zero blank rather than dropping it to the bottom of the scale, and the note says how many that was. The from time matters more here than elsewhere: a stiff run's first steps are femtoseconds, and without it fifteen decades of an empty axis push the interesting part into the last inch.

A time you ask for is not usually a time the solver stopped at, and nothing is invented to fill the gap: the nearest profile that was actually computed is drawn, the legend names the time it belongs to rather than the time you asked for, and the note under the toolbar says which times moved. Late in a stiff run the steps are wide — tens of thousands of seconds on the built-in model — so ask for 10 hours there and you may well get 10.8.

Taking the run away with you

CSV writes the species ticked in the list, at the cell the chart is showing, a row per stored step. View in Data Browser writes the whole run as an HDF5 file and opens it in the Data Browser in another tab, without the file ever reaching the disk; HDF5 is the same file as a download. Both need a run behind them, and neither sends anything anywhere: the file is built in this browser and handed to a page of the same origin.

Inside it, /time is the clock in the model's own unit — it converts nothing, any more than the page does — and /Species holds every species where the chart was looking: the batch, or the cell and rock layer the panel had chosen. A column run also has /Cells/cell_007/… for every cell and /Cells/cell_007_rock_03/… for the rock behind it, so a species searched for by name in the browser comes back as one curve per cell. /Grid says where each of those cells is, /Ends/left/… and /Ends/right/… what crosses each end of the column, /Settings what the model was compiled with, and /Model/source keeps the model text itself, a line per value. The cells past a semi-infinite end are under /Cells too, after the column's own, and say so. A dual-porosity model can have more cells than a file should hold datasets, and past four thousand of them the rock layers are left out — the root attribute matrix_written says so when that happens, and the water is always there.

PHREEQC in the page

PHREEQC does what this page does not. It has a thermodynamic database, activity coefficients, ion pairs, minerals and saturation, and it speciates a water from its analysis. This page instead integrates stiff networks of reactions, with transport, in one implicit system. The two are joined both ways here. A water goes from PHREEQC into the model, and a run goes back through PHREEQC to be read. PHREEQC itself runs in the page: the U.S. Geological Survey's program, compiled to run in the browser. Nothing is uploaded. It is fetched the first time it is used: 2.4 MB, 0.8 MB as it travels.

A water from PHREEQC

From PHREEQC… on the Model tab opens a panel that takes PHREEQC input. That is a SOLUTION, and anything PHREEQC should do to it first: EQUILIBRIUM_PHASES to put it at calcite and a CO2 pressure, a REACTION, a MIX. PHREEQC calculates it, and Write the model turns the water, as PHREEQC leaves it, into the model text:

  • every aqueous species above the floor, at PHREEQC's molality, in <SPECIES>;
  • every complexation and acid–base reaction among them, as the database writes it, in <EQUILIBRIUM>;
  • with Hold the valence states at one pe, the default, each species the database writes with electrons tied to the electron by that reaction, with the database's own log K (see Redox: the electron above); without it, no reaction with electrons, so that each valence state keeps its own total and electrons move only through the reactions you write.

Held at one pe, the water is the one PHREEQC's next step makes. PHREEQC keeps the valence states a SOLUTION is given, whatever its pe says — sulfate given, there is no sulfide in it, however reducing — and any reaction then puts every couple on one pe, the electrons kept, and forms the valence states it was given none of. Where the input ends on a SOLUTION as given, it is mixed with nothing else and that water written; the report says how far its pe moved. With the floor at 0 every species PHREEQC lists is written, even one it finds none of — PHREEQC counts nothing below 10−30 — at none and with the database's log K: oxygen in a reducing water, ferric iron's complexes. A water that oxygen will enter needs them.

Following the ionic strength, the default, writes ACTIVITY = debye-huckel at the water's TEMPERATURE, each species' -gamma from the database as a= and b=, and each relation with its water and with the database's own log K. Either way the page checks the start: it compiles the text and runs its own speciation, and the report under the panel says how close that lands to PHREEQC's molalities.

The equilibria during the run says what keeps the relations on equilibrium once the run starts. Held exactly, the default, writes EQUILIBRATE = always, and each relation is an algebraic equation in every cell all through the run (see <EQUILIBRIUM> above). On equilibrium at the start only writes EQUILIBRATE = 1, and only the reactions you add move the species after that.

Held as fast reversible pairs writes EQUILIBRATE = 1 and gives each relation a kf, with the backward constant kb = kf/K. One forward constant for every relation would not do: water and carbonate need constants about thirteen orders apart to move at the same speed. So each relation gets the kf that makes it relax back to equilibrium at the rate given, at this water. Near equilibrium a relation relaxes at kf times the product of its reactants times Σ ν²/c over its species, and that is solved for kf. The kf are per second, so change TIME_UNIT and they have to change with it.

Keep the phases at saturation, with the relations held exactly, carries the input's EQUILIBRIUM_PHASES into the model. Each phase becomes a mineral (see Minerals above) at the amount PHREEQC left of it, per kilogram of water. Its relation is the phase's reaction from the database, with its log K at the water's temperature plus the saturation index it is held at, so a gas such as CO2(g) -3.5 keeps its partial pressure while any of it is left. Left off, the phases only prepare the water. A phase that dissolves with electrons, such as pyrite, is carried when the valence states are held at one pe, and not otherwise; nor is one whose line says more than a saturation index and an amount. The report under the panel names both kinds.

An exchanger in the input, an EXCHANGE that PHREEQC's last calculation holds, is carried when the relations are held exactly. Its sites become an exchanger and each species PHREEQC put on it an exchange species at PHREEQC's molality (see Ion exchange above), with its relation from the database's EXCHANGE_SPECIES and the log K PHREEQC's activities give there. With the activity coefficients following the ionic strength, each species keeps the database's -gamma. A species whose coefficient PHREEQC takes from more than its one ion has it folded into its log K at this water, and the text says so. The run then starts from the exchanger PHREEQC loaded. Held as fast pairs instead, the exchanger is not carried, and the report says so.

A surface in the input, a SURFACE that PHREEQC's last calculation holds, is carried in the same way. Each species PHREEQC put on it becomes a surface species at PHREEQC's molality, the bare sites among them (see Surface complexation above), with its relation from the database's SURFACE_SPECIES and the database's log K at the water's temperature. With its diffuse double layer, PHREEQC's default, each surface's potential is written too, Hfo_psi, at PHREEQC's potential, with the area and the grams to each kilogram of water from the surface's first line; with -no_edl there is none. Not carried: a surface whose sites follow a phase or a kinetic reactant, one whose block says more than its sites and -no_edl, such as -cd_music or -diffuse_layer, and an input with surfaces both with a double layer and without. The report names what was left out, and why.

A gas phase in the input, a GAS_PHASE that PHREEQC's last calculation holds, is carried as a gas phase (see A gas phase above) when the relations are held exactly: each gas at the moles PHREEQC left in it to each kilogram of water, with its relation from the database's PHASES and PHREEQC's log K at the water's temperature and pressure, and the phase at the pressure or in the volume, to each kilogram of water, it was held at. A gas the database gives a critical temperature and pressure is a real one, and its line takes Tc=, Pc= and omega=, with each pair's binary parameter in <GAS_PAIRS> where PHREEQC's is not 0, its own for water vapour with CO2, H2S, CH4, N2, ethane and propane among them. Under a fixed volume the water is put at the gas phase's pressure as PHREEQC puts it, PRESSURE. Not carried: a gas whose dissolution takes what the water does not have, and a bubble whose other gases are then not all there; and a gas phase that mixes real gases with ideal ones.

Solid solutions in the input, a SOLID_SOLUTIONS that PHREEQC's last calculation holds, are carried when the relations are held exactly (see Solid solutions above): each end member a mineral at the moles PHREEQC left in its solid solution, to each kilogram of water, with its relation from the database's PHASES and PHREEQC's log K at the water's temperature, and for two members PHREEQC's Guggenheim parameters, written as a0 and a1 at the temperature PHREEQC holds their energies at, tc where that is not 25 °C, from whichever form the input gives them in: -Gugg_nondimensional, -Gugg_kJ, activity or distribution coefficients, a miscibility or spinodal gap, a critical or alyotropic point, Thompson and Waldbaum's or Margules's. PHREEQC reads -tempc and -temp as the degrees above 25 °C, adding 298.15 to them, and the text keeps the temperature PHREEQC used. Not carried: a solid solution with a member the database has no reaction for, or whose dissolution takes what the water does not have, or electrons where the valence states are not held at one pe; one of a single member; and Guggenheim's parameters for more than two.

A name this page cannot write is renamed, and the top of the text lists every one: (UO2)2(OH)2+2 becomes UO2_2(OH)2+2. Concentrations are molalities, so rate constants you add must use the same unit. Diffusion can give every species one coefficient, or each its own from the database's -dw; with each its own the text says DIFFUSION = nernst-planck as well, so that the ions are coupled and the water stays neutral as they spread (see Diffusion that keeps the charge).

A run through PHREEQC

The PHREEQC tab gives PHREEQC every stored water of the run, up to Times per cell of them in each cell. PHREEQC then speciates them with its own constants, activity model and ion pairs.

  • Each species the database has, under the same name or the renamed one, is counted into the element totals PHREEQC takes: HCO3- into C(4), H2 twice into H(0), (UO2)2(OH)2+2 twice into U(6).
  • The pH is walked until PHREEQC's molality of H+ is the run's [H+].
  • A mineral of the model, or an end member of a solid solution, is not part of the water: it is listed with the phases, its saturation index beside theirs. Nor is an exchanger, a surface or a species on either: each is looked at whole, below.
  • The model's electron, if it has one, is PHREEQC's: each water is given the pe the run has there, and the valence states are looked at against it, below.
  • Left out, and listed with their largest concentration: water; an e- that is not the electron, the hydrated electron of a radiolysis model; radicals and whatever else the database lacks.
  • The temperature is the one the model was written at, read from the top of its text, or 25 °C for a model that does not say. Where the model's TEMPERATURE goes with the cell or the time, each water is given the one it has in the run.
  • fill in from the water, off unless ticked, takes the temperature from the water in the panel on the Model tab. It also takes any element the model does not carry at all, such as a background electrolyte, held at its totals. A model written from a water carries every element already, so this is for one that leaves its counter-ions out. A water PHREEQC will not take is said, and left out.

What comes back is the pH as an activity and the saturation indices, drawn against time for one cell, or, under Show, against distance down the column at one of the times looked at, where a front's chemistry is a shape; and a CSV with every cell, the ionic strength, the charge balance and the activity of water. It also says:

  • which phases are supersaturated, and where and when — what would precipitate if the model let it;
  • how far the activity coefficients in the model's own equilibria moved along the run, in log K units. For a model with constants fixed at the start, that is how far they have gone wrong: a few hundredths is fine, and a tenth or more means a fresh water nearer the run. A model with ACTIVITY follows them itself, and then the tab says instead how close its own activity coefficients stay to PHREEQC's along the run, relation by relation;
  • for each mineral of the model, the saturation index PHREEQC finds where the model has some of it, which holding it means is its target, and how far below saturation the water is where it has none;
  • for an exchanger of the model, how close the run's equivalent fractions are to the ones PHREEQC puts on it when it loads an exchanger of the run's capacity from the same water, species by species, at every water looked at;
  • for a surface of the model, how close the run's fractions of the sites are to the ones PHREEQC puts on it when it loads a surface with the run's sites from the same water, species by species, at every water looked at: with its double layer, the potential's area and grams given, where the model has a potential, and then how close the run's potential is to PHREEQC's as well; without electrostatics where it has none;
  • for a gas phase of the model, how close each gas's partial pressure in the run — its fugacity, for a real gas — is to the pressure PHREEQC finds the same water holds it at, 10SI, wherever the run has any of it;
  • for a solid solution of the model, how close each member's activity in the run — its mole fraction times λ, or inside a miscibility gap the gap's edge's — is to the one PHREEQC finds the same water holds the pure member at, 10SI, wherever the run has any of it;
  • for the electron of the model, the pe over the waters, and how close the run's valence states are to the ones PHREEQC shares each element out to when it is given the water at that pe with the element as one total, as a part of the element;
  • a water far from neutral, which usually means the model leaves out a counter-ion, and so PHREEQC's ionic strength is too low.

A look at a run stays on the tab after the next run, saying that it is of the run before.

The Jacobian

Every rate law is differentiated symbolically with respect to each species it mentions. Nothing is differenced, so there is no step size to choose and no accuracy to lose. The chemistry is the same in every cell, so the matrix is one block per cell plus the transport terms, and those couple a species only to itself next door — which is why the state is ordered cell by cell rather than species by species: the matrix comes out banded instead of spread across its whole width.

The Jacobian tab draws the pattern of that matrix: the chemistry of each cell in the accent colour, the transport couplings to its two neighbours in the text colour, which is what puts the two thin diagonals either side of the blocks. In batch mode there is one block and it fills the picture. Nothing about the drawing is a guess — the worker folds the real pattern into a cell against itself and against each neighbour, which is the whole of it, and the caption says how much of each block is actually there.

Check Jacobian compares it against a central difference. Read the result with one thing in mind: an entry far smaller than the largest in its column cannot be measured that way at all — the change it makes is lost under the change the big ones make — so those are counted separately rather than called wrong.

Choosing a solver

NDF is the default and is the right first choice. Its BDF formulas switch, under Advanced settings, runs the same integrator with the κ terms off. The menu is Kompartment's, under each method's own name, and the canister radiolysis page shares it.

Auto starts on an explicit method — Tsit5, or Vern7 below a relative tolerance of 10−5, at a tenth of the tolerances — watches every step for stiffness as DifferentialEquations.jl's default does, and hands the rest of the run to the NDF once it finds it. A model that never turns stiff, A → B → C or the Brusselator, stays explicit and costs less than on the NDF; a transport column turns stiff in its first steps and costs what the NDF does. Rosenbrock 2-3 and Dormand–Prince 4-5 are Kompartment's own one-step methods: the first forms its Jacobian and factorises it every step — once a run, on a model whose Jacobian cannot change, such as a tracer column or a decay chain — and the second is explicit, for a model that is not stiff. The Julia ports are independent implementations of other methods: FBDF is the one to try on a large transport problem, RadauIIA5 when two others disagree, DefaultODEAlgorithm to compare with Julia's own default, and Tsit5 and Vern7 for a model that is not stiff.

Two settings come from Kompartment too. First step sets the step the run starts with, where the derivative at the start does not say what is about to happen; 0 lets the solver choose. Norm control, the NDF's, judges the error against the size of the whole solution rather than each species in each cell against its own, as MATLAB's NormControl does — looser wherever the species differ by orders of magnitude, so it is off.

Every one of them is available wherever the page is: a run normally happens on a background worker, and where there is no worker to be had — a page opened straight off the disk cannot start one — it happens on the page's own thread instead. The answer is the same; what you lose is the progress bar and the Stop button, because the page cannot repaint until the run is over. Serving the folder rather than opening the file gets both back: python3 -m http.server in it, then the address it prints.

If NDF will not get through a model, try FBDF before anything else. The kind of chemistry this page is for is hard on a corrector: with rate constants up to 1016 against concentrations of 10-9, the terms of a rate balance cancel over sixteen digits and the residual cannot be worked out to better than about a part in 107. A Newton correction that has reached that floor stops shrinking, which an ordinary convergence test reads as divergence. This page tells the solver to take such a correction when it is within half the tolerance and the Jacobian is the one for that point; without it, a reaction set of this kind can take millions of steps to cover a few seconds, and with it runs in a few thousand.

Where the built-in model came from

Water radiolysis and uranium dissolution at a spent-fuel surface: 127 reactions over 36 species, converted mechanically from the skbrtm example set. Radiolysis enters as a source of G × dose rate, with G in mol/J and the dose rate in Gy/s, so with a litre of water weighing a kilogram the product is mol/L/s and no conversion factor is needed. The dose rate is written as a constant on each radiolysis line, which holds it uniform across the film — the real one is not, it falls away over the range of an alpha particle, about 30 µm. To put that profile in, move it into a parameter and let every radiolysis line use it:

<PARAMETERS>
DOSE  all  0.64*exp(-x/3.0E-5)

then delete the DOSE = 0.64 from the ends of those lines, so that the name resolves to the parameter instead.
