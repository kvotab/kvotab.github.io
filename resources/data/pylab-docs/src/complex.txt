@ real, imag
The real and imaginary parts of complex numbers.

For a real array, `real` gives it back and `imag` gives zeros. Together with `abs` and `angle` they take complex numbers apart; `complex` puts them together.

# Syntax
x = real(z)
    The real parts.
y = imag(z)
    The imaginary parts.
# Examples
## The parts of some numbers
z = [3+4i, -2i, 5];
real(z)
imag(z)
## The roots of a quadratic, split into parts
r = roots([1 2 5]);
[real(r) imag(r)]
# See also
abs angle complex conj

@ conj
The complex conjugate.

The imaginary part changes sign: `conj(a + bi)` is `a - bi`. `z.*conj(z)` is the squared modulus, and the conjugate transpose `A'` conjugates too.

# Syntax
w = conj(z)
    The conjugate of each element.
# Examples
## Conjugates and the squared modulus
z = 3 + 4i;
conj(z)
z * conj(z)
# See also
real imag ctranspose

@ angle
The phase angle of a complex number, in radians between -pi and pi.

The angle from the positive real axis to the number in the complex plane: `angle(z)` is `atan2(imag(z), real(z))`. With `abs`, it gives the polar form `z = abs(z)*exp(1i*angle(z))`.

# Syntax
theta = angle(z)
    The phase of each element.
# Examples
## Phases, and the polar form back
z = [1, 1i, -1, -1i, 1+1i];
angle(z)
w = 2 - 2i;
abs(w) * exp(1i*angle(w))

@ complex
A complex number from its real and imaginary parts (kept complex when the imaginary part is 0).

`complex(a, b)` is `a + b*1i`, except that the result stays complex even when `b` is zero, where `a + 0i` would be stored as real. `complex(a)` makes a real array complex.

# Syntax
z = complex(a, b)
    Real parts `a`, imaginary parts `b`.
z = complex(a)
    `a` as complex numbers with zero imaginary parts.
# Examples
## From parts, and kept complex
z = complex([1 2], [3 4])
w = complex(5, 0);
isreal(w)
isreal(5 + 0i)
# See also
real imag isreal

@ isreal
True when an array is stored without imaginary parts.

It tests how the array is stored, not its values: `complex(5, 0)` is not real by this test though its imaginary part is zero. Arithmetic that gives zero imaginary parts usually gives a real result.

# Syntax
tf = isreal(A)
    True when `A` has no imaginary parts.
# Examples
## Stored as real or as complex
isreal([1 2 3])
isreal(sqrt(-1))
isreal(complex(1, 0))

@ cplxpair
Sort complex numbers into conjugate pairs, the real ones last.

The pairs come in order of increasing real part, the number with the negative imaginary part first in each; the real numbers follow, sorted. Numbers that have no conjugate are an error. Used to tidy roots and poles, which come in such pairs for real polynomials.

# Syntax
B = cplxpair(A)
    The numbers of `A` in conjugate pairs.
B = cplxpair(A, tol)
    Pairs within the tolerance `tol`.
# Examples
## The roots of a real polynomial, paired
r = roots([1 3 4 8 2 6])';
cplxpair(r)

@ unwrap
Remove the jumps of 2*pi from a sequence of phase angles.

Angles from `angle` or `atan2` stay between -pi and pi, so a steadily turning phase jumps back by 2*pi each turn. `unwrap` adds multiples of 2*pi wherever two neighbours differ by more than pi (or `tol`), giving a smooth phase.

# Syntax
q = unwrap(p)
    The phases without their jumps.
q = unwrap(p, tol)
    With jumps larger than `tol` removed.
q = unwrap(p, tol, dim)
    Along dimension `dim`.
# Examples
## A phase that turns three times
t = linspace(0, 3, 300);
p = angle(exp(1i*2*pi*t));
plot(t, p, t, unwrap(p))
legend('angle', 'unwrapped', 'Location', 'northwest')

@ cart2pol, pol2cart
Cartesian to polar (or cylindrical) coordinates, and back.

`cart2pol` gives the angle `theta` (in radians, from the x axis) and the distance `rho` from the origin; `pol2cart` goes back. A third coordinate `z` passes through unchanged (cylindrical coordinates).

# Syntax
[theta, rho] = cart2pol(x, y)
    The polar coordinates of the points (x, y).
[theta, rho, z] = cart2pol(x, y, z)
    Cylindrical coordinates.
[x, y] = pol2cart(theta, rho)
    The Cartesian coordinates of polar points.
[x, y, z] = pol2cart(theta, rho, z)
    From cylindrical coordinates.
# Examples
## A point both ways
[theta, rho] = cart2pol(1, 1)
[x, y] = pol2cart(theta, rho)
## Points on a circle
[x, y] = pol2cart(linspace(0, 2*pi, 7), 2);
round([x; y], 4)
# See also
cart2sph atan2 hypot

@ cart2sph, sph2cart
Cartesian to spherical coordinates, and back.

`cart2sph` gives the azimuth (the angle in the x-y plane, from the x axis), the elevation (the angle up from that plane) and the distance from the origin, the angles in radians; `sph2cart` goes back.

# Syntax
[azimuth, elevation, r] = cart2sph(x, y, z)
    Spherical coordinates of the points.
[x, y, z] = sph2cart(azimuth, elevation, r)
    Cartesian coordinates.
# Examples
## A point both ways
[az, el, r] = cart2sph(1, 1, sqrt(2))
[x, y, z] = sph2cart(az, el, r)
