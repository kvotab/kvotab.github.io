@ gamma, gammaln
The gamma function, and its logarithm.

`gamma(x)` is the integral from 0 to infinity of `t^(x-1)*exp(-t)`; for whole numbers `gamma(n+1)` is `n!`, and it fills in the values between them. It has poles at 0 and the negative whole numbers, and overflows above about 171; `gammaln` gives `log(gamma(x))` for positive `x` without overflowing, for ratios of large factorials.

# Syntax
y = gamma(x)
    The gamma function of each element.
y = gammaln(x)
    Its logarithm, for x > 0.
# Examples
## Factorials, and a half-integer
gamma(1:6)
[gamma(0.5)^2 pi]
## The curve and its poles
x = linspace(-4, 5, 2000);
y = gamma(x);
y(abs(y) > 10) = NaN;
plot(x, y, 1:5, factorial(0:4), 'o')
ylim([-10 10]), grid on
## Where gamma overflows
[gamma(200) gammaln(200) sum(log(1:199))]
# See also
factorial beta psi gammainc

@ gammainc, gammaincinv
The regularized incomplete gamma function, and its inverse.

`gammainc(x, a)` is the integral of `t^(a-1)*exp(-t)` from 0 to `x`, divided by `gamma(a)`: the cumulative distribution function of the gamma distribution with shape `a`, rising from 0 to 1. `'upper'` gives `1 - gammainc(x, a)` accurately in the tail; the scaled forms multiply by `gamma(a+1)*exp(x)/x^a`. Note the order: `x` first, then `a`. `gammaincinv(y, a)` gives the `x` with `gammainc(x, a) = y`.

# Syntax
y = gammainc(x, a)
    The lower function.
y = gammainc(x, a, 'upper')
    The upper function.
y = gammainc(x, a, 'scaledlower')
    Scaled (also `'scaledupper'`).
x = gammaincinv(y, a)
    The inverse (also with `'upper'`).
# Examples
## Curves for three shapes
x = linspace(0, 10, 200);
plot(x, gammainc(x, 1), x, gammainc(x, 2), x, gammainc(x, 5))
legend('a = 1', 'a = 2', 'a = 5', 'Location', 'southeast'), grid on
## A Poisson probability, and a median
> The probability of at most 3 events when 2 are expected is the upper function at x = 2, a = 4.
[gammainc(2, 4, 'upper') poisscdf(3, 2)]
m = gammaincinv(0.5, 3)
gammainc(m, 3)
# See also
gamma gamcdf betainc

@ beta, betaln
The beta function, and its logarithm.

`beta(z, w)` is the integral from 0 to 1 of `t^(z-1)*(1-t)^(w-1)`, equal to `gamma(z)*gamma(w)/gamma(z+w)`. For large arguments it underflows to 0; `betaln` gives its logarithm.

# Syntax
y = beta(z, w)
    The beta function.
y = betaln(z, w)
    Its logarithm.
# Examples
## The function and its relation to gamma
[beta(2, 3) gamma(2)*gamma(3)/gamma(5)]
[beta(500, 600) betaln(500, 600)]
# See also
gamma betainc

@ betainc, betaincinv
The regularized incomplete beta function, and its inverse.

`betainc(x, z, w)` is the integral of `t^(z-1)*(1-t)^(w-1)` from 0 to `x`, divided by `beta(z, w)`: the cumulative distribution function of the beta distribution. `'upper'` gives `1 - betainc(x, z, w)` accurately; `betaincinv(y, z, w)` gives the `x` with `betainc(x, z, w) = y`.

# Syntax
y = betainc(x, z, w)
    The lower function.
y = betainc(x, z, w, 'upper')
    The upper function.
x = betaincinv(y, z, w)
    The inverse.
# Examples
## A binomial tail, and a median
> The chance of at least 7 successes in 10 trials with p = 0.5 is betainc(0.5, 7, 4).
[betainc(0.5, 7, 4) 1 - binocdf(6, 10, 0.5)]
m = betaincinv(0.5, 2, 3)
## Curves for three shapes
x = linspace(0, 1, 200);
plot(x, betainc(x, 2, 5), x, betainc(x, 2, 2), x, betainc(x, 5, 2))
legend('2, 5', '2, 2', '5, 2', 'Location', 'southeast'), grid on
# See also
beta betacdf gammainc

@ erf, erfc, erfcx
The error function, its complement, and the scaled complement.

`erf(x)` is `2/sqrt(pi)` times the integral of `exp(-t^2)` from 0 to `x`: the chance that a normal value lies within `x*sqrt(2)` standard deviations of the mean. `erfc(x)` is `1 - erf(x)`, computed so that it stays accurate where `erf(x)` is nearly 1; `erfcx(x)` is `exp(x^2)*erfc(x)`, which neither overflows nor underflows for large `x`.

# Syntax
y = erf(x)
    The error function.
y = erfc(x)
    The complementary error function.
y = erfcx(x)
    The scaled complementary error function.
# Examples
## Within one, two and three standard deviations
erf((1:3)/sqrt(2))
## In the tail
x = [3 6 10];
fprintf('1 - erf: %10.3e %10.3e %10.3e\n', 1 - erf(x))
fprintf('erfc:    %10.3e %10.3e %10.3e\n', erfc(x))
[erfcx(30) exp(30^2)*erfc(30)]
## The curves
x = linspace(-3, 3, 200);
plot(x, erf(x), x, erfc(x)), grid on
legend('erf', 'erfc', 'Location', 'east')
# See also
erfinv normcdf

@ erfinv, erfcinv
The inverse error function, and the inverse complementary error function.

`erfinv(y)` gives the `x` with `erf(x) = y` for y in [-1, 1]; `erfcinv(y)` the `x` with `erfc(x) = y` for y in [0, 2], accurate when `y` is tiny, where `1 - y` would round to 1. Times `sqrt(2)`, they give normal quantiles.

# Syntax
x = erfinv(y)
    The inverse error function.
x = erfcinv(y)
    The inverse complementary error function.
# Examples
## The normal 95% bounds, two ways
[sqrt(2)*erfinv(0.95) sqrt(2)*erfcinv(0.05)]
## Far in the tail
[erfinv(1 - 1e-20) erfcinv(1e-20)]
# See also
erf norminv

@ expint
The exponential integral E1(x).

The integral of `exp(-t)/t` from `x` to infinity: large near 0 and falling off like `exp(-x)/x`. Negative and complex arguments give the analytic continuation, complex on the negative axis. In groundwater hydraulics it is the well function of the Theis solution.

# Syntax
y = expint(x)
    E1 of each element.
# Examples
## A few values
expint([0.1 1 5])
expint(-1)
## Drawdown around a pumped well (Theis)
> s = Q/(4πT)·E1(r²S/(4Tt)), here 10 m from a well pumped at 10 L/s, transmissivity 1e-3 m²/s, storativity 1e-4.
Q = 0.01; T = 1e-3; S = 1e-4; r = 10;
t = logspace(1, 6, 100);
s = Q/(4*pi*T)*expint(r^2*S./(4*T*t));
semilogx(t, s), grid on
xlabel('time (s)'), ylabel('drawdown (m)')

sinint cosint gammainc

@ psi
The digamma function (the derivative of gammaln), or its higher derivatives.

`psi(x)` is `gamma'(x)/gamma(x)`; `psi(k, x)` is its k-th derivative (the trigamma function for k = 1). At whole numbers, `psi(n+1)` is the harmonic number `1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/n` minus Euler's constant.

# Syntax
y = psi(x)
    The digamma function.
y = psi(k, x)
    Its k-th derivative.
# Examples
## Euler's constant, a harmonic number, and pi^2/6
-psi(1)
psi(5) - psi(1)
[psi(1, 1) pi^2/6]
# See also
gamma gammaln

@ besselj, bessely
Bessel functions of the first and second kind, J and Y.

The solutions of Bessel's equation `x^2*y'' + x*y' + (x^2 - nu^2)*y = 0`, which appear in waves and diffusion in circles and cylinders. `J` is finite at 0; `Y` goes to minus infinity there. Both oscillate and decay like `1/sqrt(x)`. The order `nu` need not be whole, and a row of orders with a column of points gives a table; with 1 as the third input, the result is scaled by `exp(-abs(imag(z)))`.

# Syntax
J = besselj(nu, z)
    The first kind.
J = besselj(nu, z, 1)
    Scaled.
Y = bessely(nu, z)
    The second kind.
# Examples
## J0, J1 and J2
x = linspace(0, 20, 400);
plot(x, besselj(0, x), x, besselj(1, x), x, besselj(2, x)), grid on
legend('J_0', 'J_1', 'J_2')
## Y0 and Y1
x = linspace(0.1, 20, 400);
plot(x, bessely(0, x), x, bessely(1, x)), grid on
ylim([-2 1]), legend('Y_0', 'Y_1', 'Location', 'southeast')
## The first zero of J0, where a drumhead's lowest mode has its rim
fzero(@(x) besselj(0, x), [2 3])
# See also
besseli besselk besselh airy

@ besseli, besselk
Modified Bessel functions of the first and second kind, I and K.

The solutions of `x^2*y'' + x*y' - (x^2 + nu^2)*y = 0`: `I` grows like `exp(x)/sqrt(x)` and `K` decays like `exp(-x)/sqrt(x)`, as in heat conduction and diffusion around cylinders. With 1 as the third input they are scaled, `I` by `exp(-abs(real(z)))` and `K` by `exp(z)`, to stay finite far out.

# Syntax
I = besseli(nu, z)
    The first kind.
K = besselk(nu, z)
    The second kind.
I = besseli(nu, z, 1)
    Scaled (also for `besselk`).
# Examples
## The two kinds
x = linspace(0.05, 3, 300);
plot(x, besseli(0, x), x, besseli(1, x), x, besselk(0, x), x, besselk(1, x)), grid on
ylim([0 5]), legend('I_0', 'I_1', 'K_0', 'K_1')
## Scaled where the plain one overflows
[besseli(0, 800) besseli(0, 800, 1)]
# See also
besselj bessely

@ besselh
The Hankel functions, Bessel functions of the third kind.

`H1 = J + iY` and `H2 = J - iY`, waves travelling outward and inward from a cylinder. `besselh(nu, z)` is the first kind; `besselh(nu, k, z)` takes the kind `k` (1 or 2).

# Syntax
H = besselh(nu, z)
    The first kind.
H = besselh(nu, k, z)
    The kind `k`.
# Examples
## Built from J and Y
besselh(0, 1, 2)
besselj(0, 2) + 1i*bessely(0, 2)
besselh(0, 2, 2)
# See also
besselj bessely

@ airy
The Airy functions Ai and Bi, and their derivatives.

The solutions of `y'' = x*y`: oscillating for negative `x`, and for positive `x` decaying (`Ai`) or growing (`Bi`). They describe light near a caustic and a quantum particle in a uniform field. `k` selects 0 for Ai, 1 for Ai', 2 for Bi and 3 for Bi'; with 1 as the third input the results are scaled.

# Syntax
W = airy(z)
    Ai.
W = airy(k, z)
    Ai, Ai', Bi or Bi'.
W = airy(k, z, 1)
    Scaled.
# Examples
## Ai and Bi
x = linspace(-10, 2, 400);
plot(x, airy(0, x), x, airy(2, x)), grid on
ylim([-0.6 1.2]), legend('Ai', 'Bi', 'Location', 'northwest')
## Values at zero
[airy(0, 0) airy(1, 0) airy(2, 0) airy(3, 0)]

@ ellipke
Complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kind, K(m) and E(m).

`K(m)` is the integral from 0 to `pi/2` of `1/sqrt(1 - m*sin(t)^2)` and `E(m)` of `sqrt(1 - m*sin(t)^2)`, for the parameter m in [0, 1]. They give a pendulum's period at any amplitude and the perimeter of an ellipse.

# Syntax
K = ellipke(m)
    The first kind.
[K, E] = ellipke(m)
    Both kinds.
# Examples
## A pendulum's period, against the small-swing period
> A pendulum swinging out to θ0 takes 2K(m)/π times the small-swing period, with m = sin²(θ0/2).
theta0 = [10 30 60 90 120 150];
ratio = 2*ellipke(sind(theta0/2).^2)/pi
## The perimeter of an ellipse
> With semi-axes a and b, the perimeter is 4a·E(1 - b²/a²).
a = 2; b = 1;
[~, E] = ellipke(1 - b^2/a^2);
P = 4*a*E
# See also
ellipj

@ ellipj
The Jacobi elliptic functions sn, cn and dn.

For the parameter m in [0, 1]: `sn(u)` and `cn(u)` are the sine and cosine of the amplitude `phi` where the incomplete elliptic integral of the first kind reaches `u`, and `dn = sqrt(1 - m*sn^2)`. With m = 0 they are `sin`, `cos` and 1; they have period `4K(m)`, and describe the swing of a pendulum at large amplitude.

# Syntax
[sn, cn, dn] = ellipj(u, m)
    The three functions.
# Examples
## One period at m = 0.9
m = 0.9;
K = ellipke(m);
u = linspace(0, 4*K, 400);
[sn, cn, dn] = ellipj(u, m);
plot(u, sn, u, cn, u, dn), grid on
legend('sn', 'cn', 'dn', 'Location', 'southwest')
## Identities
[sn, cn, dn] = ellipj(0.7, 0.4);
[sn^2 + cn^2, dn^2 + 0.4*sn^2]
# See also
ellipke

@ legendre
Associated Legendre functions of degree n, for each order m from 0 to n.

A row for each order m = 0, 1, ..., n and a column for each `x` in [-1, 1]: `P(1, :)` is the Legendre polynomial of degree n. `'sch'` gives the Schmidt semi-normalized functions, used for geomagnetic fields, and `'norm'` the fully normalized ones.

@ sinint, cosint
The sine and cosine integrals.

`sinint(x)` is the integral from 0 to `x` of `sin(t)/t`, tending to `pi/2`; `cosint(x)` is Euler's constant plus `log(x)` plus the integral from 0 to `x` of `(cos(t) - 1)/t`, tending to 0, and complex for negative `x`. Symbolic input gives the symbolic functions.

@ dawson
Dawson's integral.

`exp(-x^2)` times the integral from 0 to `x` of `exp(t^2)`. It rises to a maximum of about 0.541 near x = 0.924 and then falls like `1/(2x)`; it appears in the line shapes of spectroscopy and in plasma physics.

# Syntax
Y = dawson(X)
    Dawson's integral of each element.
# Examples
## The curve and its maximum
x = linspace(0, 6, 300);
plot(x, dawson(x), x(20:end), 1./(2*x(20:end)), ':'), grid on
legend('dawson', '1/(2x)')
[xm, fneg] = fminbnd(@(x) -dawson(x), 0, 2);
[xm -fneg]

@ fresnels, fresnelc
The Fresnel sine and cosine integrals.

`fresnels(x)` is the integral from 0 to `x` of `sin(pi*t^2/2)` and `fresnelc(x)` of `cos(pi*t^2/2)`; both tend to 1/2. Drawn against each other they make the Euler (Cornu) spiral, a curve whose curvature grows in step with its length, used for road and railway transitions and in diffraction at an edge.

# Syntax
Y = fresnels(X)
    The Fresnel sine integral.
Y = fresnelc(X)
    The Fresnel cosine integral.
# Examples
## The Euler spiral
t = linspace(-5, 5, 1000);
plot(fresnelc(t), fresnels(t)), axis equal, grid on
## Slowly toward one half
> Far out they oscillate within about 1/(πx) of 1/2.
x = [10 100 1000];
[fresnelc(x); fresnels(x)]
