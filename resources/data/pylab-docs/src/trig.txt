@ sec, csc, cot
The secant, cosecant and cotangent of angles in radians: 1/cos, 1/sin and 1/tan.

Element by element. Where the cosine, sine or tangent is zero they are infinite (or, because pi is not exact, very large).

# Syntax
y = sec(x)
    1./cos(x).
y = csc(x)
    1./sin(x).
y = cot(x)
    1./tan(x).
# Examples
## Some values
x = [pi/3 pi/4 pi/6];
[sec(x); csc(x); cot(x)]
## The secant's curve, between its poles
x = linspace(-1.4, 1.4, 200);
plot(x, sec(x))
grid on
# See also
sin cos tan secd asec

@ asin, acos, atan
The inverse sine, cosine and tangent, in radians.

`asin` gives angles from -pi/2 to pi/2, `acos` from 0 to pi, `atan` from -pi/2 to pi/2. Outside [-1, 1] the inverse sine and cosine are complex. For the angle of a point (x, y) in all four quadrants, use `atan2`.

# Syntax
y = asin(x)
    The angle whose sine is `x`.
y = acos(x)
    The angle whose cosine is `x`.
y = atan(x)
    The angle whose tangent is `x`.
# Examples
## Back to the angles
asin(0.5)
acos([1 0 -1])
atan(1) * 4
## Outside [-1, 1]
asin(2)
# See also
sin atan2 asind

@ atan2, atan2d
The angle of the point (x, y): in radians between -pi and pi, or in degrees between -180 and 180.

Unlike `atan(y/x)`, which cannot tell (1, 1) from (-1, -1), `atan2(y, x)` uses the signs of both to give the angle in the right quadrant. Note the order: `y` first.

# Syntax
theta = atan2(y, x)
    In radians.
theta = atan2d(y, x)
    In degrees.
# Examples
## The four quadrants
x = [1 -1 -1 1];
y = [1 1 -1 -1];
atan2d(y, x)
atand(y ./ x)
## The direction of a vector, in radians
atan2(3, -4)
# See also
atan angle cart2pol

@ asec, acsc, acot
The inverse secant, cosecant and cotangent, in radians.

`asec(x)` is `acos(1./x)`, `acsc(x)` is `asin(1./x)` and `acot(x)` is `atan(1./x)`.

# Syntax
y = asec(x)
    The angle whose secant is `x`.
y = acsc(x)
    The angle whose cosecant is `x`.
y = acot(x)
    The angle whose cotangent is `x`.
# Examples
## Inverses of known values
asec(2)
acsc(2)
acot(1)
# See also
sec acos asecd

@ sinh, cosh, tanh
The hyperbolic sine, cosine and tangent.

`sinh(x) = (e^x - e^-x)/2`, `cosh(x) = (e^x + e^-x)/2` and `tanh = sinh/cosh`. A hanging chain takes the shape of `cosh`, and `tanh` rises smoothly from -1 to 1, a common smooth step.

# Syntax
y = sinh(x)
    The hyperbolic sine.
y = cosh(x)
    The hyperbolic cosine.
y = tanh(x)
    The hyperbolic tangent.
# Examples
## The identity cosh^2 - sinh^2 = 1
x = [0 0.5 2];
cosh(x).^2 - sinh(x).^2
## The three curves
x = linspace(-2, 2, 200);
plot(x, sinh(x), x, cosh(x), x, tanh(x))
legend('sinh', 'cosh', 'tanh', 'Location', 'north')
grid on
# See also
asinh exp sech

@ sech, csch, coth
The hyperbolic secant, cosecant and cotangent: 1/cosh, 1/sinh and 1/tanh.

`sech` is a bell-shaped curve with its peak 1 at 0; `csch` and `coth` are infinite at 0.

# Syntax
y = sech(x)
    1./cosh(x).
y = csch(x)
    1./sinh(x).
y = coth(x)
    1./tanh(x).
# Examples
## Some values
x = [0.5 1 2];
[sech(x); csch(x); coth(x)]
# See also
cosh asech

@ asinh, acosh, atanh
The inverse hyperbolic sine, cosine and tangent.

`asinh(x) = log(x + sqrt(x^2 + 1))`, defined for every real number; `acosh` is real for `x >= 1` and `atanh` for -1 < x < 1, complex elsewhere.

# Syntax
y = asinh(x)
    The inverse hyperbolic sine.
y = acosh(x)
    The inverse hyperbolic cosine.
y = atanh(x)
    The inverse hyperbolic tangent.
# Examples
## Back again
asinh(sinh(1.5))
acosh([1 2 10])
atanh(0.5)
# See also
sinh log

@ asech, acsch, acoth
The inverse hyperbolic secant, cosecant and cotangent.

`asech(x)` is `acosh(1./x)`, `acsch(x)` is `asinh(1./x)` and `acoth(x)` is `atanh(1./x)`.

# Syntax
y = asech(x)
    The inverse hyperbolic secant.
y = acsch(x)
    The inverse hyperbolic cosecant.
y = acoth(x)
    The inverse hyperbolic cotangent.
# Examples
## Inverses of the hyperbolic functions
asech(sech(0.7))
acsch(2)
acoth(2)
# See also
sech acosh

@ sind, cosd, tand, secd, cscd, cotd
The trigonometric functions of angles in degrees.

They give exact results where the values are exact: `sind(180)` is exactly 0 and `cosd(90)` exactly 0, while `sin(pi)` is about 1.2e-16 because `pi` is not exact.

# Syntax
y = sind(x)
    The sine of `x` degrees.
y = cosd(x)
    The cosine.
y = tand(x)
    The tangent.
y = secd(x)
    The secant.
y = cscd(x)
    The cosecant.
y = cotd(x)
    The cotangent.
# Examples
## Exact zeros
sind(180)
sin(pi)
cosd([0 60 90 180])
## A table of angles
d = [0 30 45 60];
[sind(d); cosd(d); tand(d)]
# See also
sin deg2rad asind

@ asind, acosd, atand, asecd, acscd, acotd
The inverse trigonometric functions, in degrees.

The same as `asin`, `acos` and the rest, with the angle given in degrees.

# Syntax
y = asind(x)
    The angle in degrees whose sine is `x`.
y = acosd(x)
    Whose cosine is `x`.
y = atand(x)
    Whose tangent is `x`.
y = asecd(x)
    Whose secant is `x`.
y = acscd(x)
    Whose cosecant is `x`.
y = acotd(x)
    Whose cotangent is `x`.
# Examples
## Angles in degrees
asind(0.5)
acosd([1 0.5 0])
atand(1)
asecd(2)
# See also
sind atan2d asin

@ sinpi, cospi
sin(pi*x) and cos(pi*x), exact at the integers and half-integers.

`sinpi(x)` is exactly 0 at the integers and `cospi(x)` exactly 0 at the half-integers, where `sin(pi*x)` and `cos(pi*x)` leave round-off.

# Syntax
y = sinpi(x)
    sin(pi*x).
y = cospi(x)
    cos(pi*x).
# Examples
## Exact zeros
sinpi([0 1 2 0.5])
sin(pi*[0 1 2 0.5])
cospi(0.5)
cos(pi/2)
# See also
sin sind

@ deg2rad, rad2deg
Degrees to radians, and radians to degrees.

`deg2rad(d)` is `d*pi/180` and `rad2deg(r)` is `r*180/pi`.

# Syntax
r = deg2rad(d)
    Degrees as radians.
d = rad2deg(r)
    Radians as degrees.
# Examples
## Both ways
deg2rad([90 180 360])
rad2deg(pi/4)
# See also
sind cosd
