@ sym
A symbolic number, variable or matrix: exact values that formulas are built from.

`sym('x')` is a variable; arithmetic on it builds expressions that stay exact (`1/3` stays a third, `sqrt(2)` stays a root) until `double` or `vpa` gives numbers. A double becomes the exact number it stands for when it is a ratio p/q, p·π/q or √p/q to the last bit, else its binary fraction (`'f'`), or a decimal (`'d'`). Variables are complex unless assumed `'real'`, `'positive'`, `'integer'` or `'rational'`. `sym('a', [2 3])` makes a matrix of variables named `a1_1`, `a1_2`, .... Symbolic values take the operators, indexing and the elementary and matrix functions numbers do.

# Syntax
x = sym('x')
    A variable.
x = sym('x', 'real')
    With an assumption.
A = sym('a', [2 3])
    A matrix of variables.
v = sym(0.1)
    A number made exact (here 1/10).
v = sym(M, flag)
    By `'r'` (ratios, the default), `'f'` (the binary fraction) or `'d'` (a decimal).
# Examples
## Exact numbers
sym(1/3) + sym(1/6)
sym(0.1)
sym(0.1, 'f')
sqrt(sym(8))
## A matrix of variables and its determinant
A = sym('a', [2 2])
det(A)
# See also
syms vpa double subs

@ syms
Symbolic variables and functions made in the workspace.

A shorter way to make variables: `syms x y` is `x = sym('x'); y = sym('y')`, with assumptions after the names (`real`, `positive`, `integer`, `rational`). `syms f(x)` makes x and a symbolic function f of x, to be given a formula (`f(x) = x^2`) or left unknown for `dsolve`. Alone, `syms` lists the symbolic variables.

# Syntax
syms x y z
    Variables.
syms a b positive
    With an assumption.
syms f(x) g(x, y)
    Symbolic functions.
syms a [1 3]
    A vector of variables a1, a2, a3.
syms x clear
    Assumptions on x removed.
syms
    The list.
# Examples
## Variables, a function and the list
syms x y positive
syms f(x)
f(x) = x^2 + y;
f(3)
syms a [1 3]
a
syms
# See also
sym symfun assume

@ str2sym
A symbolic expression or equation from text.

The text is read in the language's syntax: `^` and `*` as powers and products, `==` as an equation, functions such as `sin` and `exp` as their symbolic forms.

# Syntax
e = str2sym(text)
    The expression or equation.
# Examples
## From text
e = str2sym('x^2 + sin(y)')
eq = str2sym('x + y == 3')
solve(eq, sym('x'))
# See also
sym char

@ symfun, argnames, formula
Symbolic functions: a formula with its inputs, and its parts.

A symbolic function is called with values for its inputs: `f(2, 3)`. It is made by `symfun(expr, [x y])`, or by assigning, `f(x, y) = expr`. `argnames` gives its inputs, `formula` its formula as an expression (of a fit or fittype, the model as text).

# Syntax
f = symfun(expr, [x y])
    A symbolic function.
v = argnames(f)
    Its inputs.
e = formula(f)
    Its formula.
# Examples
## Made, called and taken apart
syms x y
f = symfun(x^2 + y, [x y])
f(2, 3)
argnames(f)
formula(f)
# See also
syms matlabFunction

@ vpa, digits
Numbers to many significant digits: variable-precision arithmetic.

`vpa(x)` evaluates a symbolic value to `digits` significant digits (32 to begin with); `vpa(x, d)` to `d`. A double is first recognized as `sym` recognizes it, so `vpa(pi)` is π itself, not the double nearest it.

# Syntax
v = vpa(x)
    To the current number of digits.
v = vpa(x, d)
    To `d` digits.
digits(d)
    The number of digits set.
d = digits
    The current number.
# Examples
## Pi and a root to 30 digits, a seventh to 10
vpa(pi, 30)
vpa(sqrt(sym(2)), 30)
old = digits(10);
vpa(sym(1)/7)
digits(old);
# See also
sym double

@ subs
Values or expressions put in for symbolic variables.

`subs(e, old, new)` replaces `old` (a variable, an expression such as `diff(y(t), t)`, or an array of them) with `new` (numbers or expressions of the same size, or a cell). An array of values for one variable gives an array of results. `subs(e)` takes the variables' values from the workspace.

# Syntax
s = subs(e, old, new)
    One replacement, or arrays of them.
s = subs(e, {x, y}, {1, 2})
    Several, in cells.
s = subs(e, new)
    For the first variable of `e`.
s = subs(e)
    From the workspace.
# Examples
## Numbers, an array, and an expression
syms x y
e = x^2 + y;
subs(e, x, 3)
subs(e, {x, y}, {1, 2})
subs(e, x, [1 2 3])
subs(e, y, sin(x))
# See also
vpa double matlabFunction

@ simplify, simplifyFraction
A simpler form of a symbolic expression, and a fraction with its common factors cancelled.

`simplify` tries rewritings and keeps the shortest result; `'IgnoreAnalyticConstraints'` also allows rules that hold only for positive reals, such as `log(a*b) = log(a) + log(b)`. `simplifyFraction` writes a rational expression as one fraction in lowest terms. For a graph, `simplify` merges parallel edges and removes self-loops.

# Syntax
s = simplify(e)
    Simplified.
s = simplify(e, 'IgnoreAnalyticConstraints', true)
    With rules for positive reals.
s = simplifyFraction(e)
    One fraction, cancelled.
H = simplify(G)
    A graph with one edge for each joined pair.
# Examples
## Identities and fractions
> For a complex x, sqrt(x^2) is not x (take x = -1); the rules for positive reals make it so.
syms x
simplify(sin(x)^2 + cos(x)^2)
simplify(sqrt(x^2))
simplify(sqrt(x^2), 'IgnoreAnalyticConstraints', true)
simplify(log(exp(x)), 'IgnoreAnalyticConstraints', true)
simplifyFraction((x^2 - 1)/(x^2 + 2*x + 1))
# See also
expand collect rewrite

@ expand, collect, combine, horner
Expressions multiplied out, gathered by powers, combined by function, and nested.

`expand` multiplies out products and powers and expands functions of sums (`sin(a + b)`); `collect` gathers the terms by powers of a variable; `combine` joins terms of one kind (`'powers'` unless given, `'log'`, `'sincos'`, `'exp'`, ...); `horner` writes a polynomial in nested form, cheapest to evaluate. For datastores, `combine` reads several together.

# Syntax
s = expand(e)
    Multiplied out.
s = collect(e, x)
    By powers of `x`.
s = combine(e, target)
    Terms of one kind combined.
s = horner(p)
    Nested.
dsnew = combine(ds1, ds2, ...)
    Datastores read side by side.
# Examples
## Four forms of the same expressions
> Logarithms combine only where that is true: here for positive a and b.
syms x y
syms a b positive
expand((x + y)^3)
expand(sin(a + b))
collect(x*y + x^2*y + 3*x, x)
combine(log(a) + log(b), 'log')
horner(x^3 - 2*x^2 + 3*x - 4)
# See also
simplify factor rewrite

@ rewrite
An expression rewritten in terms of other functions.

`rewrite(e, 'exp')` writes the trigonometric and hyperbolic functions with exponentials; other targets are `'log'`, `'sin'`, `'cos'`, `'tan'`, `'sqrt'`, `'sincos'`, `'sinhcosh'`, the inverse functions, `'heaviside'` and `'piecewise'`.

# Syntax
s = rewrite(e, target)
    In terms of the target.
# Examples
## The same functions, other forms
syms x
rewrite(cos(x), 'exp')
rewrite(tan(x), 'sincos')
rewrite(asinh(x), 'log')
# See also
simplify expand

@ partfrac, numden
Partial fractions of a rational expression, and its numerator and denominator.

`partfrac(e, x)` writes a ratio of polynomials in `x` as a polynomial plus simple fractions; `'FactorMode', 'full'` factors the denominator over the complex numbers. `numden` gives the numerator and denominator after putting the expression over one denominator.

# Syntax
s = partfrac(e, x)
    Partial fractions.
s = partfrac(e, x, 'FactorMode', 'full')
    With the denominator fully factored.
[n, d] = numden(e)
    Numerator and denominator.
# Examples
## A fraction parted and put back
syms x
partfrac((2*x + 3)/(x^2 + 3*x + 2), x)
[n, d] = numden(1/x + 1/(x + 1))
# See also
residue simplifyFraction

@ coeffs, poly2sym, sym2poly
The coefficients of a symbolic polynomial, and polynomials between coefficients and symbolic form.

`coeffs(p, x)` gives the nonzero coefficients from the lowest power up, and the terms with them; with `'All'`, every coefficient from the highest power down, the order `polyval` uses. `poly2sym` makes a symbolic polynomial from coefficients; `sym2poly` gives a polynomial's coefficients as doubles.

# Syntax
[c, t] = coeffs(p, x)
    Nonzero coefficients and their terms.
c = coeffs(p, x, 'All')
    All of them, highest power first.
p = poly2sym(c, x)
    A symbolic polynomial.
c = sym2poly(p)
    Its coefficients as doubles.
# Examples
## A polynomial both ways
syms x a
p = 3*x^3 + a*x + 5;
[c, t] = coeffs(p, x)
coeffs(p, x, 'All')
q = poly2sym([1 -2 3], x)
sym2poly(q)
# See also
polyval roots

@ children, has
The parts of a symbolic expression, and whether one is in it.

`children` gives the operands of the top operation: the terms of a sum, the factors of a product, the arguments of a function, the sides of an equation. `has(e, sub)` is true when `sub` appears in `e`.

# Syntax
c = children(e)
    The operands, in a cell array.
tf = has(e, sub)
    Whether `sub` is in `e`.
# Examples
## A sum taken apart
syms x y
e = x^2 + sin(y) + 3;
c = children(e);
c{:}
[has(e, sin(y)) has(e, cos(y))]

The left and right sides of a symbolic equation or inequality.

L = lhs(eqn)

R = rhs(eqn)

@ isolate
An equation rearranged so that a variable, or an expression such as a derivative, stands alone on the left.

# Syntax
e = isolate(eqn, x)
    For a variable.
e = isolate(eqn, expr)
    For an expression: `diff(y(t), t, 2)`, `x^2`, ....
# Examples
## For a variable, and for an acceleration
syms a b c x
isolate(a*x + b == c, x)
syms theta(t) L g

@ finverse, compose
The inverse of a symbolic function, and functions composed; or data formatted into strings.

`finverse(f)` gives g with g(f(x)) = x, where one exists on the principal branch. `compose(f, g)` is f(g(x)). With a format and data instead, `compose` formats each row of values into a string, as `sprintf` does, one string per row.

# Syntax
g = finverse(f)
    The inverse, in the same variable.
g = finverse(f, x)
    In `x`.
h = compose(f, g)
    `f(g(x))`.
s = compose(format, a1, a2, ...)
    Formatted strings, one per row.
# Examples
## Inverse and composition
syms x
finverse(exp(2*x) + 1)
compose(sin(x), x^2)
## Strings from rows of values
compose('%s: %d', ["a"; "b"], [1; 2])

subs sprintf

@ int
Integrals of symbolic expressions, indefinite or definite.

`int(f, x)` gives an antiderivative (without a constant); `int(f, x, a, b)` the definite integral, where the limits may be symbolic or infinite. The variable is `symvar(f, 1)` unless given. An integral with no closed form is left as `int(...)`; `'Hold', true` keeps it so.

# Syntax
F = int(f, x)
    An antiderivative.
F = int(f, x, a, b)
    From `a` to `b`.
F = int(..., 'Hold', true)
    Kept unevaluated.
# Examples
## Antiderivatives and definite integrals
syms x a
int(x*exp(x), x)
int(1/(1 + x^2), x, 0, Inf)
int(exp(-a*x^2), x, -Inf, Inf)
int(sin(x)/x, x, 0, Inf)

diff integral symsum

@ limit
The limit of a symbolic expression as a variable approaches a point.

From both sides unless `'left'` or `'right'` is given; when the two differ the limit is NaN. The point may be infinite.

# Syntax
L = limit(f, x, a)
    As `x` approaches `a`.
L = limit(f, x, a, 'left')
    From the left (or `'right'`).
L = limit(f)
    At 0, in `symvar(f, 1)`.
# Examples
## Classic limits, and one sided
syms x n
limit(sin(x)/x, x, 0)
limit((1 + 1/n)^n, n, Inf)
[limit(1/x, x, 0, 'left') limit(1/x, x, 0, 'right') limit(1/x, x, 0)]
# See also
taylor diff

@ taylor, series
Taylor polynomials and series of a symbolic expression about a point.

`taylor(f, x, a)` gives the terms up to the power n - 1 (`'Order'`, 6 unless given) about `a` (0 unless given). `series` gives the series with negative and fractional powers too, where the expression has them (Laurent and Puiseux series). For dynamic models, `series(sys1, sys2)` connects them one after the other.

# Syntax
T = taylor(f, x)
    About 0.
T = taylor(f, x, a, 'Order', n)
    About `a`, to order `n`.
S = series(f, x, a)
    With negative or fractional powers where needed.
sys = series(sys1, sys2)
    Two models in series.
# Examples
## A polynomial approximation, and a Laurent series
syms x
T = taylor(exp(x), x, 'Order', 4)
series(1/sin(x), x, 'Order', 4)
fplot([exp(x) T], [-2 2]), legend('exp(x)', 'Taylor, order 4', 'Location', 'northwest')

limit diff

@ symsum, symprod
Symbolic sums and products, finite or infinite.

`symsum(f, k, a, b)` sums f over k from a to b, which may be infinite; `symsum(f, k)` is the indefinite sum, F with F(k+1) - F(k) = f(k). `symprod` multiplies.

# Syntax
F = symsum(f, k, a, b)
    A sum.
F = symsum(f, k)
    The indefinite sum.
F = symprod(f, k, a, b)
    A product.
# Examples
## Sums and a product in closed form
syms k n
symsum(k^2, k, 1, n)
symsum(1/k^2, k, 1, Inf)
symprod(1 + 1/k, k, 1, n)
symprod(1 - 1/k^2, k, 2, Inf)
