@ ttest
The one-sample or paired t-test: is the mean m (0 unless given)?

Tests whether normal data could have the mean `m`, from their sample mean and standard deviation. Given two samples of the same length, it tests the differences x - y (a paired test). `h` is 1 when the test rejects the mean at the level alpha (5%), `p` is the p-value, `ci` the confidence interval of the mean, and `stats` holds the t statistic, its degrees of freedom and the standard deviation. A matrix is tested column by column.

# Syntax
h = ttest(x)
    Is the mean 0?
h = ttest(x, m)
    Is the mean `m`?
h = ttest(x, y)
    Is the mean of `x - y` 0 (paired data)?
[h, p, ci, stats] = ttest(..., 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', 'both' | 'right' | 'left')
    With the p-value, interval and statistics; another level, or a one-sided test.
# Examples
## A sample against a mean
x = [5.1 4.9 5.6 5.8 6.0 5.4 5.7 5.3];
[h, p, ci, stats] = ttest(x, 5)
## Before and after, paired
before = [72 80 68 75 90 66];
after = [70 76 69 71 84 64];
[h, p] = ttest(before, after)
# See also
ttest2 ztest signrank tinv

@ ttest2
The two-sample t-test: do two samples have the same mean?

Compares the means of two independent normal samples. By default the variances are taken to be equal; `'Vartype', 'unequal'` gives Welch's test, which does not. `ci` is the interval of mean(x) - mean(y).

# Syntax
h = ttest2(x, y)
    Do the means differ?
[h, p, ci, stats] = ttest2(x, y, 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', tail, 'Vartype', 'equal' | 'unequal')
    With the p-value, interval and statistics, and the options.
# Examples
## Two groups
a = [12.1 11.8 12.6 12.4 11.9 12.2];
b = [12.9 13.1 12.5 13.4 12.8];
[h, p, ci] = ttest2(a, b)
[~, pWelch] = ttest2(a, b, 'Vartype', 'unequal')
# See also
ttest ranksum anova1

@ ztest
The z-test: is the mean m, for data whose standard deviation sigma is known?

Like the t-test, with the standard deviation known instead of estimated, so the normal distribution gives the p-value.

# Syntax
h = ztest(x, m, sigma)
    Is the mean `m`?
[h, p, ci, zval] = ztest(x, m, sigma, 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', tail)
    With the p-value, interval and z statistic.
# Examples
## A process with a known spread
x = [10.3 10.1 10.6 10.4 10.2 10.5];
[h, p, ci, z] = ztest(x, 10, 0.3)
# See also
ttest normcdf

@ vartest, vartest2
Tests of variances: is the variance of normal data v, and do two normal samples have the same variance?

`vartest` uses the chi-square distribution of the sample variance; `vartest2` the F distribution of the ratio of two variances. Both depend on the data being normal.

# Syntax
h = vartest(x, v)
    Is the variance `v`?
[h, p, ci, stats] = vartest(x, v, 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', tail)
    With the p-value, the interval of the variance and the statistic.
h = vartest2(x, y)
    Are the two variances equal?
[h, p, ci, stats] = vartest2(x, y, 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', tail)
    With the p-value and the interval of var(x)/var(y).
# Examples
## A variance, and two
rng default
x = normrnd(0, 1.3, 40, 1);
[h, p, ci] = vartest(x, 1)
y = normrnd(0, 1, 40, 1);
[h2, p2] = vartest2(x, y)
# See also
vartestn ttest2

@ vartestn
A test of equal variances in several groups.

Bartlett's test by default (for normal data); Levene's and the Brown-Forsythe test are less sensitive to departures from normality. The groups are the columns of a matrix, or the values of a vector by a grouping variable. A summary of the groups is shown unless `'Display'` is `'off'`.

# Syntax
p = vartestn(X)
    The columns of `X` as the groups.
p = vartestn(x, group)
    The values of `x` by group.
[p, stats] = vartestn(..., 'TestType', type, 'Display', 'off')
    `'Bartlett'`, `'LeveneAbsolute'`, `'LeveneQuadratic'`, `'BrownForsythe'` or `'OBrien'`.
# Examples
## Three groups, two tests
rng default
X = [normrnd(0, 1, 30, 1), normrnd(0, 1, 30, 1), normrnd(0, 2, 30, 1)];
p = vartestn(X, 'Display', 'off')
p2 = vartestn(X, 'TestType', 'BrownForsythe', 'Display', 'off')
# See also
vartest2 anova1

@ signtest, signrank
Tests of a median: the sign test and Wilcoxon's signed rank test.

Both ask whether the median is `m` (0 unless given), or for paired data whether the median of x - y is 0, without assuming normal data. The sign test counts the values above and below; the signed rank test also uses how far they lie, and is more powerful for symmetric data. The p-value comes first, then `h`.

# Syntax
p = signtest(x, m)
    The sign test.
p = signrank(x, m)
    The signed rank test.
p = signrank(x, y)
    Paired data.
[p, h, stats] = signrank(..., 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', tail, 'Method', 'exact' | 'approximate')
    With `h`, the statistics and options (and the same for `signtest`).
# Examples
## A median, both ways
x = [1.8 2.4 2.9 3.1 3.6 4.0 4.4 5.2 6.1];
[signtest(x, 2) signrank(x, 2)]
## Paired data
before = [72 80 68 75 90 66 71];
after = [70 76 69 71 84 64 70];
[p, h, stats] = signrank(before, after)
# See also
ttest ranksum

@ ranksum
The Wilcoxon rank sum test (Mann-Whitney U): do two samples come from distributions with the same median?

Ranks all the values together and asks whether one sample's ranks are higher than chance would give: a test of two independent samples without assuming normal data. Exact for small samples, a normal approximation otherwise.

# Syntax
p = ranksum(x, y)
    The p-value.
[p, h, stats] = ranksum(x, y, 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', tail, 'Method', 'exact' | 'approximate')
    With `h` and the statistics.
# Examples
## Two small samples
a = [3.1 2.7 4.0 3.5 2.9];
b = [4.6 3.9 5.2 4.8 4.4 5.0];
[p, h, stats] = ranksum(a, b)
# See also
ttest2 kruskalwallis signrank

@ kstest, kstest2
Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests: do data come from a given continuous distribution, and do two samples come from the same one?

The statistic is the largest distance between distribution functions: the sample's and the given one (the standard normal unless given), or the two samples'. The given distribution's parameters must not be estimated from the same data (for that, `lillietest`).

# Syntax
h = kstest(x)
    Against the standard normal.
[h, p, ksstat, cv] = kstest(x, 'CDF', pd, 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', tail)
    Against a distribution object.
h = kstest2(x1, x2)
    Two samples.
[h, p, ks2stat] = kstest2(x1, x2, 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', tail)
    With the p-value and statistic.
# Examples
## Normal, and not
rng default
x = randn(100, 1);
[h, p] = kstest(x)
[h2, p2] = kstest(exprnd(1, 100, 1), 'CDF', makedist('Normal', 'mu', 1, 'sigma', 1))
## Two samples
rng default
[h, p] = kstest2(randn(80, 1), 0.5 + randn(80, 1))
# See also
lillietest adtest chi2gof

@ lillietest
The Lilliefors test: are the data normal (or exponential, or extreme value), with the parameters estimated from them?

The Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic against the normal distribution with the sample's own mean and standard deviation, with the critical values that estimating them requires.

# Syntax
h = lillietest(x)
    Are the data normal?
[h, p, kstat, cv] = lillietest(x, 'Alpha', alpha, 'Distr', 'norm' | 'exp' | 'ev')
    With the p-value, statistic and critical value; another family.
# Examples
## Normal and skewed data
rng default
[h, p] = lillietest(normrnd(5, 2, 60, 1))
[h, p] = lillietest(exprnd(2, 60, 1))
# See also
kstest jbtest adtest

@ jbtest
The Jarque-Bera test of normality, from the sample's skewness and kurtosis.

Normal data have skewness 0 and kurtosis 3; the statistic measures how far the sample's are from these.

# Syntax
h = jbtest(x)
    Are the data normal?
[h, p, jbstat, critval] = jbtest(x, alpha)
    With the p-value, statistic and critical value.
# Examples
## Normal and heavy-tailed data
rng default
[h, p] = jbtest(randn(200, 1))
[h, p] = jbtest(trnd(2, 200, 1))

@ adtest
The Anderson-Darling test: do the data come from a family of distributions, or from one distribution?

Like the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, but weighting the tails more, so it is more sensitive to departures there. With a family's name the parameters are estimated from the data; with a distribution object they are known.

# Syntax
h = adtest(x)
    Are the data normal?
[h, p, adstat, cv] = adtest(x, 'Distribution', 'norm' | 'exp' | 'ev' | 'logn' | 'weibull' | pd, 'Alpha', alpha)
    Another family, or a distribution object.
# Examples
## Lognormal data tested as normal and as lognormal
rng default
x = lognrnd(0, 0.6, 100, 1);
[h1, p1] = adtest(x)
[h2, p2] = adtest(x, 'Distribution', 'logn')
# See also
lillietest kstest

@ chi2gof
The chi-square goodness-of-fit test, on the data counted in bins.

Compares the counts in bins with the counts a distribution expects. Without a distribution the data are tested for normality, the mean and standard deviation estimated from them; bins expected to hold fewer than 5 values join their neighbours. `stats` has the statistic, its degrees of freedom, the bins and the observed and expected counts.

# Syntax
h = chi2gof(x)
    Are the data normal?
[h, p, stats] = chi2gof(x, 'CDF', pd, 'NBins', n, 'Edges', edges, 'Alpha', alpha)
    Against a distribution, with bins of your own.
[...] = chi2gof(x, 'Ctrs', c, 'Frequency', f, 'Expected', E, 'NParams', k)
    Counts given with their expected values, a bin centred on each value of `c`.
# Examples
## Is a die fair?
> 120 rolls, counted by face, against 20 each.
counts = [25 18 21 14 23 19];
[h, p, stats] = chi2gof(1:6, 'Ctrs', 1:6, 'Frequency', counts, 'Expected', 20*ones(1, 6));
[h p stats.chi2stat stats.df]
## Normal data
rng default
[h, p] = chi2gof(randn(300, 1))

@ runstest
The runs test: do the values above and below a reference (their median unless given) come in random order?

A run is a stretch of values on one side of the reference. Too few runs mean values cluster (a trend or a cycle); too many mean they alternate. Values equal to the reference are left out.

# Syntax
h = runstest(x)
    About the median.
h = runstest(x, v)
    About the value `v`.
[h, p, stats] = runstest(..., 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', tail, 'Method', 'exact' | 'approximate')
    With the p-value and the counts of runs.
# Examples
## Random order, and a trend
rng default
[h, p] = runstest(randn(50, 1))
[h, p, stats] = runstest((1:50)' + 5*randn(50, 1))

signtest autocorr

@ anova1
One-way analysis of variance: do several groups have the same mean?

Splits the variation of the data into the part between the groups' means and the part within the groups, and tests their ratio with the F distribution. The groups are the columns of a matrix, or the values of a vector by group. The table is shown in the Command Window and the groups' box plots in a figure, unless the last input is `'off'`; `stats` goes to `multcompare` to see which groups differ.

# Syntax
p = anova1(X)
    The columns of `X` as the groups.
p = anova1(x, group)
    The values of `x` by group.
[p, tbl, stats] = anova1(..., 'off')
    The table and statistics, nothing shown.
# Examples
## Three groups, the table and the box plots
y = [5.2 5.8 6.1 5.5 6.6 7.1 6.8 7.4 5.1 4.9 5.4 5.0];
g = {'A' 'A' 'A' 'A' 'B' 'B' 'B' 'B' 'C' 'C' 'C' 'C'};
p = anova1(y, g)
# See also
multcompare anova2 kruskalwallis ttest2

@ anova2
Two-way analysis of variance of a balanced layout: the columns are one factor, the rows (in blocks of reps) the other.

Tests the column factor, the row factor and, with more than one replicate per cell (`reps`), their interaction. The p-values come in that order.

# Syntax
p = anova2(Y)
    One observation per cell.
p = anova2(Y, reps)
    `reps` rows per level of the row factor.
[p, tbl, stats] = anova2(Y, reps, 'off')
    The table and statistics, nothing shown.
# Examples
## Two factors with two replicates
> Columns: three fertilizers; rows: two soils, two plots each.
Y = [20 24 26; 21 25 27; 18 23 29; 17 22 30];
p = anova2(Y, 2, 'off')
# See also

@ kruskalwallis
The Kruskal-Wallis test: do several groups come from distributions with the same median?

The one-way analysis of variance of ranks, for groups whose data may not be normal. The statistic is corrected for ties and tested with the chi-square distribution. Shown as a table and box plots unless the last input is `'off'`.

# Syntax
p = kruskalwallis(X)
    The columns of `X` as the groups.
p = kruskalwallis(x, group)
    The values of `x` by group.
[p, tbl, stats] = kruskalwallis(..., 'off')
    Nothing shown; `stats` for `multcompare`.
# Examples
## Three skewed groups
rng default
X = [exprnd(1, 20, 1), exprnd(1, 20, 1), exprnd(2.5, 20, 1)];
p = kruskalwallis(X, [], 'off')

@ friedman
Friedman's test: do the columns differ, the rows (blocks) taken out?

The two-way analysis of variance of ranks within blocks: each row (a judge, a subject, a day) ranks the columns (the treatments), and the test asks whether some columns rank higher throughout.

# Syntax
p = friedman(X)
    One observation per cell.
p = friedman(X, reps)
    `reps` rows per block.
[p, tbl, stats] = friedman(X, reps, 'off')
    Nothing shown.
# Examples
## Four judges rank three wines
X = [7 9 8; 6 8 7; 5 9 6; 6 7 8];
p = friedman(X, 1, 'off')

@ multcompare
Which pairs of groups differ, after anova1, kruskalwallis or friedman.

Each row of the result compares two groups: their numbers, the interval of the difference (lower end, estimate, upper end) and the p-value, adjusted for the number of comparisons (Tukey-Kramer by default). The figure shows each group's estimate with an interval: groups whose intervals do not overlap differ.

# Syntax
c = multcompare(stats)
    The comparisons, and the figure.
[c, m, h, gnames] = multcompare(stats, 'Alpha', alpha, 'CType', type, 'Display', 'off')
    With the groups' means and names; another adjustment; no figure.
# Examples
## After a one-way analysis of variance
y = [5.2 5.8 6.1 5.5 6.6 7.1 6.8 7.4 5.1 4.9 5.4 5.0];
g = {'A' 'A' 'A' 'A' 'B' 'B' 'B' 'B' 'C' 'C' 'C' 'C'};
[~, ~, stats] = anova1(y, g, 'off');
c = multcompare(stats, 'Display', 'off')

@ crosstab
A table of counts by two or more grouping variables, with the chi-square test of their independence.

`tbl(i, j)` counts the observations in group i of the first variable and group j of the second. The chi-square statistic and its p-value test whether the two are independent; `labels` names the groups.

# Syntax
tbl = crosstab(g1, g2)
    The table of counts.
[tbl, chi2, p, labels] = crosstab(g1, g2, ...)
    With the test and the groups' names.
# Examples
## Two variables of eight people
smoker = {'yes' 'no' 'no' 'yes' 'no' 'no' 'yes' 'no'};
cough = {'yes' 'no' 'yes' 'yes' 'no' 'no' 'yes' 'no'};
[tbl, chi2, p, labels] = crosstab(smoker, cough)

@ fishertest
Fisher's exact test of independence in a 2-by-2 table of counts.

Exact, so it suits small counts where the chi-square test's approximation fails. `stats` has the odds ratio and its confidence interval.

# Syntax
h = fishertest(x)
    Are the rows and columns independent?
[h, p, stats] = fishertest(x, 'Alpha', alpha, 'Tail', tail)
    With the p-value and the odds ratio.
# Examples
## A small table
x = [8 2; 1 5];
[h, p, stats] = fishertest(x)
